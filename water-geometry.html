The geometry of water

Water is one small shape, two hooks and two loops, and every drop is a crowd of them. How far does that geometry explain drops, ice, stone and still water?

Water is one small molecule with two hooks and two loops, and every drop is a crowd of them, each holding its neighbours by the same local rule. How far do that shape and that rule explain what drops, ice, stone and still water do? Behaviours that look separate, a drop beading on a raincoat, ice floating, a pail freezing in a flash, may be one geometry seen at different sizes. What is known, what is still open, and which experiments would settle it.

Seven words in plain terms

  • HookOne of water's two hydrogens, slightly positive. It catches a loop. A scientist says a hydrogen-bond donor.
  • LoopOne of the oxygen's two spare pairs of electrons, slightly negative. It holds a hook. A scientist says an acceptor, or a lone pair.
  • FasteningA hook held in a loop: the hydrogen bond. Weak, best in a straight line, and in the liquid remade about every picosecond.
  • RingFastenings closing on themselves. Ice is all rings of six; the liquid is a changing crowd of fives, sixes and sevens.
  • Contact angleThe angle a drop's edge makes with the solid it sits on. On this page degrees are always angles; temperatures are written in °C.
  • SupercooledWater below 0 °C that is still liquid, because ice has not yet made a patch big enough to grow.
  • Small and fastA nanometre is a millionth of a millimetre. A picosecond is a millionth of a millionth of a second, and a femtosecond a thousandth of that.

Step 1 of 11 · on the beach at 1

The shape and the fastening

The question ↓

In shortWater is an oxygen with two hooks and two loops. A hook of one molecule catches a loop of another, and in the liquid lets go again within about a picosecond.

Water is one oxygen holding two hydrogens. Around the oxygen four arms point roughly to the corners of a tetrahedron, a pyramid of four triangles. Two arms are the hydrogens, slightly positive: call them hooks. Two are pairs of the oxygen's own electrons, slightly negative: call them loops. A hook of one molecule catches a loop of another, and that catch, the hydrogen bond, is how water holds on to water. It holds best in a straight line, oxygen to hydrogen to oxygen, with the two oxygens about 0.28 nanometres apart (a nanometre is a millionth of a millimetre). It is weak beside the bonds inside the molecule, so in the liquid a fastening lasts about a picosecond, a millionth of a millionth of a second, before its hook swings to another loop. Everything on this page is this one shape, fastening and unfastening.

The angles · 1.2

The two hooks sit 104.5 degrees apart, a little closer than the 109.5 degrees between a tetrahedron's corners, because the loops push harder than the hooks. Each hook is about a tenth of a nanometre long. These degrees are angles, as on a protractor, not temperatures.

The scientists' words · 1.3

A hook is a hydrogen-bond donor, an O–H; a loop is a hydrogen-bond acceptor, a lone pair; a fastening is a hydrogen bond; a free hook is a dangling or free OH; a ring is a hydrogen-bonded ring. The page keeps the plain words so that one vocabulary runs from the molecule to the window.

Who drew it first · 1.4

J. D. Bernal and R. H. Fowler, Cambridge, 1933: each water molecule fastened to four others in a tetrahedron. They likened water just below 4 °C to tridymite and warmer water to quartz, two forms of the silica of sand and stone, because both are networks of tetrahedra (Journal of Chemical Physics 1, 515).

The jump · 1.5

A fastening does not fade slowly: the molecule turns through a large angle in one move as its hook leaves one loop for another (Laage and Hynes, Science 2006). Measured with infrared pulses, liquid water loses the memory of its arrangement within about 50 femtoseconds, a twentieth of a picosecond (Cowan, Miller and colleagues, Nature 2005).

Are the loops two? · 1.6

Chemists still argue whether the two loops are two equal arms, the 'rabbit ears' of textbooks, or two unequal ones, one rounder and one longer, that act together (Clauss, Weinhold and colleagues 2014; Hiberty, Danovich and Shaik 2015).

Where a fastening ends · 1.7

Every count of fastenings depends on where a line is drawn: how far apart and how bent a catch may be and still count. All definitions are to some extent arbitrary; two careful ones give about 3.2 to 3.4 fastenings per molecule in a model of the liquid (Kumar, Schmidt and Skinner, Journal of Chemical Physics 2007).

Step 2 of 11 · on the beach at 1.1

The rule and the crowd

The question ↓

In shortEach molecule is an agent with one rule, and everything else is what a crowd of them does. The hooks sit a little off the tetrahedron; the evidence so far says the rule, more than the near miss, makes water odd.

Each molecule is an agent with one rule: hold up to two neighbours by its hooks and be held by up to two through its loops, each catch best in a straight line toward a corner of its tetrahedron. Nothing else steers it, and it keeps no memory of where it has been. Everything else on this page is what a crowd of such agents does, which is why behaviours that look separate can be one geometry at different sizes: the same rule meeting other molecules, air, a solid, the cold. The rule is close to the tetrahedron's but not exact: the hooks sit 104.5 degrees apart where its corners sit 109.5 apart, so the crowd can never fit itself perfectly. Whether that near miss gives water its oddities is one question here. The evidence so far points to the rule itself: a simulated water whose hooks sit exactly 109.47 degrees apart still has a temperature of greatest density, only far colder than water's 4 °C, and a model with no hydrogens at all, one atom with a rule that favours the tetrahedron, behaves like water; its makers read that as connectivity, not chemistry, deciding how water behaves.

What you have seen · 1.12

An ice cube floats; a lake freezes from the top down; rain stands in beads on a waxed car; a pail below zero turns to slush in a second. Each is this one rule in a different setting: an open network that takes more room, a crowd that is densest just above freezing, a rule with nothing to catch, a crowd finding ice's pattern all at once. Each is told in the step where its geometry lives.

The near miss in ice · 1.13

In ice the oxygens sit almost exactly at a tetrahedron's corners, yet the angle inside each molecule cannot be measured directly in ordinary ice, because its hydrogens are disordered; in the ordered ices II and IX it is about a degree wider than in vapour (Whalley, Journal of Glaciology 1978). How the mismatch is taken up has been argued since Chidambaram proposed bent fastenings in 1961; Kuhs and Lehmann (Nature 1981) found the hydrogens rocking about their oxygens and no sign of bent bonds.

Without hydrogens · 1.14

Molinero and Moore's model mW (Journal of Physical Chemistry B 2009) is one atom with a rule that favours tetrahedral angles, no hydrogens and no charges, and it reproduces water's structure, its anomalies and its phases. They concluded that it is not the nature of the interactions but the connectivity of the molecules that determines how water behaves. Silicon, germanium and silica, tetrahedral networks without water's chemistry, share several of its oddities.

How firmly the rule holds · 1.15

Tuning how strongly that one-atom rule insists on the tetrahedron, Hujo, Molinero and colleagues (2011) found the density anomaly in a middle band, where germanium, silicon and water sit, and absent at the weak end, tin, and the strong end, carbon. On that reading what matters is how firmly the rule holds: firmly enough to build open networks, loosely enough to keep breaking them.

The angle alone · 1.16

Change only the angle inside one simulated water, SPC/E, and its density maximum appears once the hooks sit at least 100 degrees apart, and moves as the angle widens (a study cited by Li, Zhang and Zhang, Chinese Physics B 2018). SPC/E itself uses exactly 109.47 degrees and has its density maximum near minus 38 °C, against real water's 4 °C (Martin Chaplin's compilation of water models).

Crowds on paper · 1.17

The crowd has been modelled as mathematics for half a century. Ben-Naim's flat 'Mercedes-Benz' water (1971) gives each disc three arms; a crowd of them shows the density maximum and freezes into an open hexagonal ice (Silverstein, Haymet and Dill, Journal of the American Chemical Society 1998). Stanley and Teixeira's percolation model (Journal of Chemical Physics 1980) reads the liquid as one space-filling network of fastenings holding tiny patches of fully fastened molecules, which are less dense. The instrument beside this step is a crowd of such discs.

Step 3 of 11 · on the beach at 1.11

Every hook in a loop: ice

The question ↓

In shortIn ice every hook holds a loop, in rings of six with open channels between them. That room, not air, is why ice floats.

Cool water far enough and every hook finds a loop. Each molecule holds four neighbours at the corners of its tetrahedron, and the fastenings close into rings of six. Seen from above, the rings make the hexagons of a snowflake; seen from the side they pucker like a row of chairs, and they leave open channels running through the crystal. Those channels are why ice floats: the same molecules take about nine per cent more room as ice than as liquid, and none of that room is air. The rule leaves one thing open: which of its two neighbours each hydrogen sits nearer. Every molecule must keep two hooks of its own, and there are so many ways to do that that ice keeps a little disorder down to the coldest temperatures at which it has been measured.

What you have seen · 1.116

Ice cubes float, and a full bottle left in the freezer bulges or cracks: the open rings take about nine per cent more room. Ice on a pond floats as a lid and keeps the water beneath it liquid. A snowflake's six arms follow its rings of six: the hexagon of the molecules shows at the size of the flake.

The ice rules · 1.112

Bernal and Fowler's rules: each oxygen keeps two hydrogens close and two far, and each fastening carries exactly one hydrogen. In 1935 Linus Pauling counted the arrangements the rules allow: about one and a half per molecule, (3/2) to the power N for N molecules. That predicted a leftover entropy of 3.37 joules per mole per kelvin; Giauque and Stout measured 3.4, give or take 0.2, the next year.

Why it floats · 1.113

The open lattice takes about 9 per cent more room than the liquid. Silicon, gallium, germanium, antimony and bismuth also expand on freezing; most of them, like ice, freeze into open networks.

Two ways to stack · 1.114

Ice's layers of puckered rings can stack two ways: hexagonal, the usual ice, or cubic, the pattern of diamond. Ice frozen from supercooled droplets mixes both at random, stacking-disordered ice (Malkin, Murray and colleagues, PNAS 2012); pure cubic ice was watched forming, molecule by molecule, on cold graphene in 2023 (Huang, Wang, Bai and colleagues, Nature).

The square grid · 1.115

The instrument beside this step plays the same rule on a flat square grid, the 'square ice' Elliott Lieb solved exactly in 1967: about 1.54 arrangements per molecule, close to Pauling's 1.5 for real ice. Turning the hydrogens round any closed loop keeps the rule, which is how the instrument reshuffles them.

Step 4 of 11 · on the beach at 1.111

Loops of loops: the liquid

The question ↓

In shortThe liquid is a crowd of rings forever forming and breaking. Cooled, it behaves as if it held two arrangements: an open one, and a dense one whose rings thread through each other.

Melt the ice and the rings open. In the liquid most molecules still hold three or four fastenings, but they keep trading them, so the network is a crowd of rings of five, six and seven that form and break; five-membered rings are among the most lasting. Cooled water behaves as if it held two arrangements at once: an open one close to ice, and a denser one in which a fifth molecule crowds into the gap. In simulations the denser one is entangled, its rings threaded through one another like links of a chain, while the open one's rings stay apart. A paper in Science this year reported evidence that the two liquids become one at a critical point near minus 63 °C and a thousand times atmospheric pressure.

What you have seen · 1.1117

A lake freezes from the top down. Below about 4 °C colder water is lighter, as more of it takes the open, ice-like arrangement, so the coldest water stays at the surface and freezes there first, while water near 4 °C, the densest, settles to the bottom.

Rings counted · 1.1112

In perfect ice every ring has six members. In the liquid the sizes spread; as it cools, rings of eight or more thin out while rings of six and seven grow, and six stays the commonest (simulation studies). Faure Beaulieu and colleagues (2026) tie rings of five to eight members to a split peak seen in X-ray scattering of cold water.

Threaded rings · 1.1113

Neophytou, Chakrabarti and Sciortino (Nature Physics 2022) found that in the high-density liquid the rings form knots and links, a trefoil knot and Hopf links among them, while in the low-density liquid they stay simple rings. A 2024 study of a network liquid found its densification driven mainly by rings becoming linked.

Two or four? · 1.1114

In 2004 Wernet, Nilsson and colleagues read X-ray absorption to mean that most molecules in the liquid hold two strong fastenings, one hook and one loop, in rings and chains, not four in a tetrahedron (Science 304, 995). Others answered that the tetrahedral network stands (Head-Gordon and Johnson, PNAS 2006). The argument ran for years.

Two liquids · 1.1115

Kim, Nilsson and colleagues (Science 2020) caught a high-density liquid turning into a low-density one, the two differing by about 20 per cent in density; You and colleagues (Science 2026) reported evidence of the critical point where they merge, near 210 kelvin and 1,000 bar.

The quartz link · 1.1116

Water and molten silica share their oddities, a temperature of greatest density among them, because both are open networks of tetrahedra; silica's sits near 1,550 °C. Bernal's likening of water to quartz has a reason.

Step 5 of 11 · on the beach at 1.1111

Hooks and loops left free: the surface

The question ↓

In shortAt the surface the network is cut. About a fifth of the top molecules hold a hook out into the air, and those hooks turn far faster than any wind can comb them.

Where water meets air the network is cut. A molecule at the top has no neighbour above, so some of its arms point out with nothing to hold. Laser measurements find that more than a fifth of the top molecules hold one hook straight out into the air, free hooks like the hairs of Velcro; others turn a free loop outwards. The layer that differs from the water beneath is about one molecule thick, and its free hooks never stand still: each swings round in less than a picosecond, several times faster than a molecule inside. The surface ripples with its own heat, so even still water is rough by about a third of a nanometre. Wind raises bigger ripples but cannot comb the hooks flat the way it flattens grass; they turn far faster than any gust. What does tie them down is a film of something else: a single layer of a fatty alcohol fastens the free hooks and turns the top of the water ice-like.

What you have seen · 1.11117

Drops on a car window stay round, and two drops that touch become one in a flash with no skin to burst: the top layer is one molecule thick and remade in less than a picosecond, so the two surfaces simply join, a bridge between them widening within a fraction of a millisecond. The merged drop holds the volume of both all but exactly: by the arithmetic of the pressure inside a curved drop, millimetre drops gain about one part in sixty million, drops ten nanometres across about one part in six hundred. A pond skater stands on the cut network; a drop of soap, crowding into the top layer, lets it sink.

How many · 1.11112

More than 20 per cent of the top molecules carry a free hook (Du, Superfine, Freysz and Shen, Physical Review Letters 1993), about a quarter by their count; work since puts about a fifth of the surface's OH arms unfastened.

How thick · 1.11113

Simulations put the zone that differs from the water beneath at 0.3 to 1 nanometre; X-ray reflection measured the heat-rippled roughness of a water surface at 0.32 nanometre (Braslau and colleagues, Physical Review Letters 1985).

How fast · 1.11114

Free hooks turn in under a picosecond, several times faster than water inside, because they hold fewer fastenings (Hsieh, Bonn and colleagues, Physical Review Letters 2011); they shed energy mainly by turning (Inoue, Tahara and colleagues, Nature Communications 2020).

Against oil · 1.11115

At an oily surface the water's fastenings with itself are weak, yet its molecules line up strongly against the oil (Scatena, Brown and Richmond, Science 2001).

On perfect ice · 1.11116

On the ideal flat face of ice, free hooks and free loops stick out in equal numbers; on the liquid their mix changes from moment to moment.

Step 6 of 11 · on the beach at 1.11111

Meeting a solid: does the spacing match?

The question ↓

In shortA solid offers hooks and loops at its own spacing. Where they fit water's own, water spreads; where nothing catches, it beads. The best seeds for ice are close fits.

A solid offers water hooks, loops, both or neither, set at its own spacings and angles. Where they fit water's own, the first layer carries the network into the solid and the water spreads: on quartz, patches of ice-like order form at room temperature; on mica in damp air a single ice-like layer lies flat with no free hooks left over. Where the spacing is wrong, water builds other rings: on copper it lays chains of five-sided rings, not hexagons. Where the solid offers nothing to catch, as with wax or a fluorinated coat, water's network turns back on itself and a drop stands up as a bead. The same match makes the best seeds for ice. Silver iodide, used to seed clouds for nearly eighty years, matches ice's spacing to within two per cent, but only its silver face holds water in ice's pattern; its iodine face rebuilds itself into a grid that breaks the match. Feldspar, the dust that seeds much of the sky's ice, starts it at small defects where its hydroxyl groups line water up. Spacing is not the whole story, since charge and defects matter too, but at this scale the question is less what a solid is made of than which hooks and loops it offers, and where.

What you have seen · 1.111117

Rain spreads in a sheet on a clean windscreen and stands in beads on a waxed bonnet. Freshly cleaned glass wets almost flat, and within hours a film of grease from the air makes drops bead on it again. An anti-fog coating offers hooks and loops so freely that breath spreads into a clear film instead of a fog of droplets. Silver iodide smoke is burned to seed clouds, and the feldspar in mineral dust seeds much of the ice in clouds.

Quartz and mica · 1.111112

Phase-sensitive laser spectroscopy found intermixed ice-like and liquid-like water at the quartz surface, its hydrogens pointing up or down with the water's acidity (Ostroverkhov, Waychunas and Shen, Physical Review Letters 2005). On mica at about 90 per cent humidity the first layer reads as ice-like with no free hooks (Miranda, Xu, Shen and Salmeron, Physical Review Letters 1998).

Copper's pentagons · 1.111113

Ice chains about a nanometre wide on copper are built from face-sharing pentagons, not hexagons, because that arrangement maximises water's hold on the metal while keeping its fastenings strong (Carrasco, Michaelides, Hodgson and colleagues, Nature Materials 2009).

Silver iodide · 1.111114

Its lattice matches ice's flat face to within 2 per cent. Atomic force imaging found that only the silver-terminated face templates ice; the iodine face reconstructs into a rectangular grid (Hütner, Balajka and colleagues, Science Advances 2025).

Feldspar · 1.111115

Potassium feldspar dominates the ice-seeding of mineral dust (Atkinson and colleagues 2013). Ice starts at its surface defects, where hydroxyl groups mediate (Kiselev and colleagues, Science 2017), and simulations name its (110) face, exposed at steps, as the template (Zhou and Piaggi 2025).

Antifreeze · 1.111116

Fish and bacteria make proteins that stop ice growing by holding cages of water whose spacing matches ice's faces, the 'anchored clathrate' (Garnham, Campbell and Davies, PNAS 2011).

Step 7 of 11 · on the beach at 1.111111

Edges, corners and pores: the solid's own shape

The question ↓

In shortOne size up, the solid's shape decides: an edge holds a drop, a narrow corner draws water in, roughness exaggerates, and the finest pores fill from damp air.

One size up, the solid's shape takes over, and three rules of pure geometry decide much of what you have watched on glass and stone. Call the contact angle the angle the water's edge makes with the solid. First, an edge holds a drop: its rim stays pinned at a sharp edge until the contact angle has turned through the angle of the edge itself. Second, a corner draws water in: water runs along a groove without limit when its contact angle is less than a right angle minus half the groove's opening, so a narrow crack wicks water that a wide one would not. Third, roughness exaggerates: it makes a wetting surface wetter and a repelling one more repelling, and where air stays trapped under a drop the drop sits partly on air. Overhangs go further: a surface built of overhanging, mushroom-like shapes repels even oils that would wet the same material when flat. And the finest pores fill from damp air alone, because the tight curve of a meniscus inside them holds water below the humidity at which it would otherwise condense. Glass and stone can offer much the same hooks and loops; stone is the one with the cracks, corners and pores.

What you have seen · 1.1111117

A drop on a car window clings, pinned at its rim, until enough rain has joined it for its weight to win; then it runs, leaving a line of pearls where its tail kept catching. Water climbs a towel or a sugar lump by corners and pores, soaks into stone and sits on glass. A raincoat sheds rain because its surface is rough and repelling, so drops sit partly on trapped air; kneel on wet grass and the pressure forces water into the weave, and it soaks through. Trees lift water up to a hundred metres because the menisci in their leaves' pores, a few nanometres across, pull far harder than the column weighs, and the fastenings hold the column together (the cohesion–tension theory, Dixon and Joly, 1894). A straw works the other way, pushed by the air, and no straw lifts water much beyond ten metres.

The edge rule · 1.1111112

Gibbs's criterion: at a sharp convex edge a contact line stays pinned for every contact angle between its own and that angle plus the edge's turn, as Oliver and colleagues showed by experiment. Pinning is why drops hang at steps, and why a texture's edges can hold a drop up.

The corner rule · 1.1111113

Concus and Finn (1969, 1974): in a wedge whose half-opening is α, a liquid whose contact angle is θ climbs without limit along the corner when θ is less than 90 degrees minus α, and stops at a finite height otherwise. In a square corner water must meet the wall at less than 45 degrees to run.

Air under the drop · 1.1111114

On a rough repelling surface a drop can sit on trapped air, the Cassie state, or fill the texture, the Wenzel state. Pressure or vibration can tip it irreversibly into the filled state, where it stays pinned (Lafuma and Quéré, Nature Materials 2003).

Overhangs · 1.1111115

Re-entrant, overhanging texture is a third lever beside chemistry and roughness: with it Tuteja, McKinley, Cohen and colleagues made surfaces that repel octane and pentane (Science 2007). A springtail's skin, with its overhanging ridges, does the same.

Pores that drink air · 1.1111116

By the Kelvin equation a curved meniscus holds water below saturation, and in pores a few nanometres across it does so far below. The equation was tested for water down to a meniscus of about 4 nanometres (Fisher, Gamble and Middlehurst, Nature 1981); below that it is still argued.

Step 8 of 11 · on the beach at 1.1111111

The pail below zero: freezing

The question ↓

In shortBelow zero, ice must first make a patch of its geometry big enough to grow. A seed or a jolt makes one; then a flash of branching ice freezes only a fraction, and the rest freezes slowly.

To freeze, water must first make a patch with ice's geometry big enough to grow rather than fall apart. In still, clean water below 0 °C such patches flicker into being and dissolve. Near minus 35 °C the first patch big enough to grow holds several hundred molecules, more when it is warmer, and small drops of pure water can stay liquid down to about minus 38 °C. A seed shortcuts the wait: a surface whose hooks and loops already sit in ice's pattern, or a jolt. When you lift a supercooled pail, the jolt stretches the water for an instant, and stretched or cavitating water is the commonest trigger known, though the details are still argued. Then branching crystals race through the pail, but only until the heat they give off warms the water between them to 0 °C: supercooled by 5 °C, about one part in sixteen freezes in that moment and the rest more slowly. Even then the first ice is not neat; its layers stack at random, hexagonal and cubic mixed.

What you have seen · 1.11111116

A bottle of water from a cold car or the freezer that turns to slush the moment it is opened or knocked is the pail in small. Freezing rain falls as supercooled drops and glazes a road the instant it lands. Clouds stay liquid well below zero until something seeds them. Snow, hail and sleet are three routes into ice: snow grows from vapour, hail grows as supercooled drops freeze onto a stone, clear and milky in turn, and sleet, as it is used in Britain, is snow partly melted on the way down, or snow and rain falling together.

The first patch · 1.11111112

Simulations put the critical patch at about 600 molecules near minus 35.5 °C (Sanz, Vega and colleagues 2013) and about 100 near minus 68 °C (Moore and Molinero, Nature 2011); on a clay surface that helps, it needs less than half the size it needs in open water (Sosso, Michaelides and colleagues 2016).

The jolt · 1.11111113

A shock or a sound wave stretches water for an instant. Extrapolations suggest stretched water colder than about minus 11 °C freezes before it tears open into bubbles (Marcolli, Scientific Reports 2017); trials with vibrated vials name stretching and bubbles as the main triggers (Wang and colleagues 2024); a shock of about 70 millibar froze supercooled water in tubes (Nature 1969).

How much freezes at once · 1.11111114

Freezing gives off about eighty times the heat that warms water by one degree, so the first flash freezes roughly the supercooling in degrees divided by eighty: about 6 per cent from minus 5 °C, 12 per cent from minus 10 °C. Water freezes in two stages, the fast branching and then the slow rest; deeply supercooled drops freeze about half in the first (Kalita and colleagues).

Born of vapour, born of liquid · 1.11111115

Snow grows from vapour, molecule by molecule, in the lattice's own shape: plates near minus 2 °C, columns near minus 5 °C, plates again near minus 15 °C, branching more the damper the air (Nakaya's diagram; Libbrecht). Hail grows from supercooled drops freezing onto it: instant freezing traps air in milky layers, and slow freezing under a wet film makes clear ones.

Step 9 of 11 · on the beach at 1.11111111

Ice's own skin

The question ↓

In shortIce has a surface of its own that loosens layer by layer as it warms. Whether that loose top, or the touching itself, makes ice slippery is still argued.

Ice has a surface of its own, not simply its inside cut open. Imaged molecule by molecule at about minus 150 °C, the top of ordinary ice is a patchwork of hexagonal and cubic stacking, and it begins to lose order at about minus 153 °C, starting along the seams between the patches. Laser measurements see the top layer disorder from about minus 90 °C and the second layer go in a step near minus 16 °C; only near minus 2 °C do droplets and films of true liquid appear. That loose top is one answer to why ice is slippery: its mobile molecules roll like ball bearings. A 2025 study answers differently: the sole itself, through the pull of its charged molecules on ice's, breaks the crystal into a liquid-like layer at the touch, even near absolute zero, though so cold that layer is thicker than honey. Snow sticks for the meniscus's reason: near 0 °C each grain carries a film of water that wets the next, and the menisci freeze into necks between the grains.

What you have seen · 1.111111117

Ice is slippery well below zero, where the old story of pressure melting fails: a skater's weight lowers ice's melting point far too little to melt it at minus twenty (Rosenberg, Physics Today 2005). Ice cubes in a glass freeze together where they touch, and snow packs into a snowball near 0 °C but stays as dust in deep cold: the films on their surfaces form necks between them, which freeze.

The picture · 1.111111112

Atomic force microscopy with a carbon-monoxide tip resolved ice's flat basal face: mixed hexagonal and cubic nanodomains in a repeating superstructure, premelting beginning at their defective boundaries (Hong, Jiang and colleagues, Nature 2024).

Layer by layer · 1.111111113

Spectroscopy and simulation together: the top layer starts to disorder around minus 90 °C, the second around minus 16 °C, droplets and films near minus 2 °C (Nagata, Bonn, Backus, Sazaki and colleagues, Accounts of Chemical Research 2019). The step at minus 16 °C was reported as bilayer-by-bilayer melting (Sánchez and colleagues, PNAS 2017).

Stripes · 1.111111114

On the ideal flat face of ice, free hooks and free loops are predicted to line up in alternating rows, Fletcher's striped surface, rather than at random (Buch, Tosatti and colleagues, PNAS 2008); helium scattering had seen hints of it.

Friction · 1.111111115

Ice friction falls from ordinary solids' 0.5 to a slippery 0.01 as its surface's mobile molecules multiply towards minus 7 °C (Weber, Bonn and colleagues 2018). Dipole forces amorphise ice at a contact, and slipperiness also needs a smooth, water-repelling partner (Atila, Sukhomlinov and Müser, Physical Review Letters 2025).

Wet and dry snow · 1.111111116

In wet snow at 0 °C the water shows as a meniscus between grains under a hand lens; dry snow has none and will not make a snowball (snow science's classes of liquid water content).

Step 10 of 11 · on the beach at 1.111111111

Still water, moving water

The question ↓

In shortThe arrangement of hooks and loops is remade about every picosecond, still or stirred. What lasts is what is dissolved in the water and what holds it.

Does still water keep an arrangement that moving water loses? Every measurement so far says the arrangement of hooks and loops does not last. Liquid water loses the memory of its own structure within about 50 femtoseconds, and each fastening is remade within about a picosecond, still or stirred; a pool left for decades is remade a million million times every second it sits. What lasts is what is in the water and what holds it. Water filtered through soil carries minerals, gases and organic matter that a still pool may not, and a still pool that slowly evaporates grows heavier, because light molecules leave first and the rarer heavy ones, with heavy hydrogen or heavy oxygen, stay behind. Where water touches a solid or fills a narrow space, the solid can hold the geometry in place, as on mica or in a pore. So far, the lasting differences between waters are what is dissolved in them, which kinds of molecule they hold, and what they touch.

What you have seen · 1.1111111116

Holy water that has stood for decades in a font and water drawn from a spring after filtering through soil can differ in what they carry: minerals, gases, living things, and the heavier molecules a standing pool keeps as it slowly evaporates. The scale inside a kettle is what a hard water carried. Their hooks and loops are remade a million million times a second either way.

Memory · 1.1111111112

Two-dimensional infrared spectroscopy shows liquid water losing its structural correlations within 50 femtoseconds (Cowan, Miller and colleagues, Nature 2005); the average fastening lives about a picosecond (Nibbering, Elsaesser and colleagues).

Heavier by evaporation · 1.1111111113

Heavier water molecules evaporate less readily, so water left standing to evaporate is enriched in deuterium and oxygen-18; lakes carry this signature worldwide (an IAEA survey of 1,257 lakes in 91 countries).

What has been claimed · 1.1111111114

Philip Ball's book (1999) gave detailed accounts of polywater and other episodes its reviewer in Nature called pathological science. The ultrafast measurements set the bar any claim of lasting structure in bulk water must clear: it would have to outlast that 50-femtosecond memory.

Where structure stays · 1.1111111115

On mica at room temperature, water grows layer by layer as flat ice-like islands 0.37 nanometres high, the height of a single layer of ice (Xu, Cao and Heath, Science 2010).

Where to start: the questions on one ladder

The question ↓

In shortLaid on one ladder of sizes, the ten questions climb from a tenth of a nanometre to the size of a drop. The tools to answer them now exist.

Laid on one ladder of sizes, the ten questions climb from a tenth of a nanometre to the size of a drop. At the bottom: where a fastening ends, how much the near miss in its angle explains, how ice counts its arrangements, whether the liquid's rings thread through each other. Then the surface: how free hooks and loops are laid out, and whether a solid's spacing sets water's first layer. Then shape: how small a corner or a pore can be before the rules of angles fail. Then change: the first patch of ice and the jolt that makes it, ice's own skin and its slipperiness, and whether stillness leaves anything behind. The tools to answer them now exist: microscopes that see single molecules and the hydrogens on them, X-ray lasers quick enough to catch supercooled water before it freezes, laser light that reads only the top layer, electron microscopes that watch ice form, and simulations trained on quantum chemistry. What the answers are worth is practical: better seeds for clouds and better clouds in climate models, surfaces that shed ice, ways to freeze living tissue without ice damage, and a clearer view of how water works its way into stone.

The tools · 1.11111111112

Atomic force microscopy with a carbon-monoxide tip, which imaged ice's surface (Hong 2024) and the growing edges of two-dimensional ice (Ma and colleagues, Nature 2020); X-ray free-electron lasers on fleeting supercooled water (You 2026); phase-sensitive and time-resolved sum-frequency spectroscopy of the top layer (Shen; Bonn; Tahara); cryogenic electron microscopy watching cubic ice form (Huang 2023); machine-learned simulations with quantum accuracy (Zhou and Piaggi 2025; Faure Beaulieu 2026).

What it is for · 1.11111111113

Mineral dust seeds much of the sky's ice and so shapes clouds and the planet's energy balance; silver iodide's seeding has long had a mixed record that its atomic picture may explain; ice on aircraft and roads is a standing hazard; antifreeze proteins point to ways of preserving organs and tissues; and freezing and thawing in its pores breaks stone apart.

After this page · 1.11111111114

The same geometry is the medium of life. Water cannot fasten to oil, so it must rearrange its network around an oily molecule, at a cost, and oily parts are pushed together: the hydrophobic effect, which gathers fats into droplets and membranes and helps fold proteins (Chandler, Nature 2005). It is the bead on wax at the size of a molecule. Water as the medium in which life's molecules organised, from fat droplets as the first bodies of cells to proteins, RNA and DNA, is the next page.
