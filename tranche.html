The TrancheBack the boats, not the beach

Five or six ventures built on the beach go to investors together. Each is a package an investor already knows how to buy: a product, the people making it, and who pays for it. None of them asks for money for the beach, and every one of them stands on it, so the money that backs them is also the money that puts the beach to work.

For nearly two centuries a British ship has been owned in sixty-four shares. A merchant could hold a few in several ships, and every share was in a ship, never in the beach it was built on. This proposal asks investors to do the same with the ventures built on the beach. Read the six steps, then answer the five decisions: David, Matthew, James and Mark first, or you.

step one of six 1

Everyone wants a package

Companies, investors and coders all want a finished thing that fits their funnel. The beach is ground, not a thing.

A finished, complete thing that fits the funnel they already run. A company asks what the product is; an investor asks what, exactly, they would be investing in; a coder hears that the beach inverts coding, and likes coding as it is. An investor's funnel takes six weeks at the least, through stages that check the people and check the thing before a cheque is written. The beach passes through none of these funnels, because it is not a package: it is ground that anything can be built on, and anyone can run a beach of their own on their own server.

What was asked

In October 2026 a contact of James Howard's, the one who takes work into companies, asked what the product is and how it would be packaged to go in. On 12 November 2026 an investor heard the whole of it, said it all sounded fantastic, and asked what they would actually be investing in. They are the same question, asked by people whose work runs on finished things.

Why the ground cannot be sold

Owning the server is not owning anything: the beach is open, its protocol is public, and anyone can run a beach of their own. An investor offered the real estate finds nothing scarce in it to own.

Keel's reading

The package is not a compromise. David's own practice in a room is to give C its form when C asks for it, by choice and minimally, then go back up to B. Silo Buster is that move made with organisations, a ten-day package that opens onto a network. The tranche is the same move made with investors: things they can buy, around people who share one ground.

step two of six 1.1

Nobody buys the ground

Decide ↓

Nobody bought the web or Android. Investors backed what was built on them.

Open ground has been funded before, but never by someone buying it. The web was given away by CERN in 1993, and the browsers and search engines built on it made the money, most of it in America. Google bought Android in 2005 and kept it open, earning from the paid services on top. Investors back the things built on open ground; the ground is paid for by those who depend on it, or by a sponsor who wants it to exist.

The web

Tim Berners-Lee made it at CERN between 1989 and 1991, and on 30 April 1993 CERN put its software in the public domain. The browsers that first reached millions came from Illinois and then from Netscape, and the money that followed, from browsers to search, was made mostly by American companies building on ground nobody owned. David's question is whether a British company could have backed that ground and kept the lead here.

Android

Google bought Android in 2005 and released it as open source in 2008; it did not close it. The money came from the layer on top, Google's own services and its store, on phones made by other companies. That is the shape here: open ground, and paid things on it.

The iFund

On 6 March 2008, at Apple's launch of the iPhone's software kit, the venture firm Kleiner Perkins announced the iFund, $100 million for companies building iPhone apps on a platform it did not own, and doubled it to $200 million in 2010. By 2011 most of those companies were building for Android too: the investment travelled with the companies, not the ground. It is the nearest precedent to this proposal, made by one large firm.

The other route

A large company funds the ground itself and keeps it open, as CERN's funders paid for the web, and as companies fund the Linux Foundation because their products run on Linux. David's version is a British company backing the beach as open ground, so the lead stays here. It is a different door from the tranche's: a sponsor rather than an investor, and slower to find.

Keel's reading

What makes the beach unbuyable is what makes each venture on it safer to buy. Because anyone can run a beach, no venture is captive to David's server, as the iFund's companies were never captive to Apple; an investor backing a venture is not betting that one host stays up. Said in the room, 'there is nothing to own' becomes 'there is nothing to be locked into'.

step four of six 1.111

What an investor holds

Decide ↓

An investor holds shares in the ventures they back, never in the beach. Separate companies, or one studio over all of them.

A share in each venture, bought on that venture's own terms, and never a share in the beach. The simplest form: each venture is its own small company, and one group of investors puts money into each. The other form is a studio, one company that holds a large part of every venture, so that an investor buys one share of all of them: a single package, at a cost.

Separate companies

Each venture a UK company of its own; the investors, as a syndicate, invest in each directly and may pick which. A venture that fails costs only its own cheque. Each is checked on its own, which is the same paperwork done five or six times.

The seed scheme

The UK's Seed Enterprise Investment Scheme gives an investor income tax relief of half of what they put in, up to £200,000 a year, in a company trading for under three years, with fewer than 25 staff and gross assets of no more than £350,000, which may raise up to £250,000 this way. Every ask in the tranche sits well inside that. A company controlled by another company cannot use it. Most investors ask for HMRC's advance assurance first. Read on gov.uk and from the British Business Bank, 12 November 2026.

A studio

One company holding a large part of each venture: investors buy into that one company and own a slice of everything, the single package they know. The cost: each founder gives up much of their own venture, and a venture the studio controls loses the seed scheme's relief.

A fund

Pooling investors' money and choosing for them is regulated work in the UK, and too heavy for a couple of hundred thousand pounds. It is named here only to set it aside.

Keel's reading

Separate companies keep everything the tranche is for: each person keeps their venture, an investor backs the ones they believe in, the seed scheme halves their risk, and one venture failing sinks no other. The package investors ask for can still be one page, the tranche's, with the ventures side by side. This is not legal or tax advice: each company's papers want an accountant who knows the scheme.

step five of six 1.1111

How the ground is fed

Decide ↓

The money goes to the ventures. Some of it can still reach the beach: by use, a pledge, a stake, and later a shared keeper.

The money goes to the ventures, and the beach still has to be kept: its servers, its protocol, and the time of the person who built it. There are four ways for some of the money to reach the ground, and they combine: each venture pays for what it uses; each pledges a small share of what it raises or earns; each gives the beach's maker a small stake for the ground it stands on; and later, once the ventures earn, they keep the ground together.

Use

Each venture pays for what it uses of the beach, through the tickets the beach already has, as any customer would. Honest and simple, and it pays only as fast as the ventures grow.

Pledge

Each venture writes into its terms a small share of what it raises, or of what it earns, for the ground. Investors read it on the page before they invest, so it is a cost they accept rather than one taken later.

Stake

The beach's maker holds a small share in each venture, as an adviser does, for the ground it stands on and the help that comes with it. It is the form investors know best, and it pays only when the venture does well.

A keeper, later

Once the ventures earn, they share the cost of keeping the ground, as companies fund the Linux Foundation because their products run on Linux. Not for the first round.

Keel's reading

Without one of these written into each venture's terms, the money also going into the ecology rests on use alone: real, but not something an investor or the beach can count. A pledge or a stake also fits first-meeting, where the network is funded by money moving between its people with no central pot: here the money moves from a venture to the one who made its ground, set off by the venture's own success.

step six of six 1.11111

The first round

Decide ↓

Each holder says yes, each venture writes one page, and the ones that are ready sail first; the rest follow in a second round.

In order: each holder says yes; each venture writes its package, one page saying what it is, who pays, what it needs and what it stands on; the tranche gets one page with the ventures side by side and why each needs so little; each company seeks the seed scheme's advance assurance; and David and James take the tranche into investor meetings with the ventures that are ready. Those not ready join a second round.

The one page

What an investor is handed: the ventures side by side, each with its product, its people, who pays, its ask and what is already built; the total; and one line on the ground, that every venture runs on the same open beach and is locked into none of it.

The funnel's clock

An investor's checks take six weeks at the least, and they fall on each venture separately. Starting with the ventures already in use means the first cheques can come while the others are still writing their pages.

The room

David's meeting protocol makes the deal in the room rather than after it; the tranche gives that room something an investor can buy, from each person present.

Keel's reading

Two things to say plainly in the room. Investors use the word tranche for money released to one company in stages, so 'one round for five companies' may land more clearly. And the strongest line is the ground's: each venture needs tens of thousands rather than millions, because the beach already carries what each would otherwise have to build. That is David's own thesis, that the cost of launching has collapsed, made concrete five times over.
