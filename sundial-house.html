The house

The test project is a detached four-bedroom house in the West Midlands, built by ten hands in a year: design and approvals through winter and spring, on site from June, keys before Christmas 2027.

What gets built

Two storeys of brick-and-block cavity wall under a tiled, trussed roof, heated by an air-source heat pump. Fifteen rooms and eighteen windows, with a driveway, patio, soakaway drainage and a car charger.

  • Ground floor: hall, living room, kitchen-diner, utility, cloakroom and study.
  • First floor: four bedrooms, two with en-suites, a family bathroom, the landing and a plant cupboard.
  • Structure: trench-fill foundations, a beam-and-block ground floor, engineered first-floor joists, a steel beam over the rear doors and roof trusses.
  • Services: underfloor heating downstairs, radiators upstairs, a heat pump with an unvented cylinder.
  • Finishes: liquid screed, engineered oak, LVT and carpet, tiled bathrooms and a fitted kitchen.

The dates that frame the year

  • Brief agreed: Monday 14 December 2026.
  • Planning permission: Tuesday 20 April 2027, with the builder appointed the next day.
  • On site: Wednesday 2 June 2027.
  • Weathertight: Thursday 9 September 2027.
  • First fix complete: Thursday 30 September 2027.
  • Planned handover: Tuesday 23 November 2027, followed by 17.6 working days of buffer.

1,1step 2 of the brief

The method

The plan was built backwards in six steps: fix the outcome, ask what must be true before it, turn each necessity into jobs, find the chain, set the start, and place every job on the clock.

  1. 1

    Fix the reference

    Keys on Friday 17 December 2027, a week before the Christmas shutdown. Every other date derives from this one.

  2. 2

    Work back by necessity

    From the keys, ask what must be true first, and why. Each answer is a because: "Keys wait for the completion certificate, because lenders and the warranty need it." Nineteen such statements carry the logic.

  3. 3

    Turn necessities into jobs

    Each state becomes jobs, each with one owner among the ten, a median duration, a safe duration and its steps. Council periods and lead times become waits; milestones become gates.

  4. 4

    Find the chain and pool the safety

    The critical path gives the logic, and levelling by crew gives the critical chain. The safety hidden in each job is taken out and pooled as one buffer at the end.

  5. 5

    Backcast the start

    Scheduling back from the keys, less the buffer, gives a start of Monday 14 December 2026. Two hundred simulated builds, with weather, lead times, late windows and inspection delays, turn that into start dates by confidence.

  6. 6

    Place every job on the sundial

    Each job sits at the smallest period of the canonical clock that holds both its start and its end. Its steps sit at the gatherings or days beneath; the address is the date and the depth is the scale.

1,1,1step 3 of the brief

The theory

Four traditions meet in the method: backcasting sets the direction, Theory of Constraints orders the necessities and finds the bottleneck, the critical path times the work, and Last Planner keeps weekly promises reliable.

  • Backcasting

    Robinson (1982), after Lovins (1976)

    Start from a desired future, then work back to now.

    Amory Lovins's "soft energy path" (Foreign Affairs, 1976) argued from a desired energy future back to present policy. John B. Robinson named the method backcasting in Energy Policy (1982) and set out its fundamentals in Futures (1990). Karl Dreborg (1996) stressed that it is normative, and John Holmberg and Karl-Henrik Robèrt (2000) built it into The Natural Step's framework.

    Its part hereThe reference and the backward order.

  • Theory of Constraints

    Goldratt (1984 to 1997)

    One constraint limits the whole; necessity trees; pooled buffers.

    Eliyahu M. Goldratt's The Goal (1984) argued that one constraint limits any system, and It's Not Luck (1994) set out the thinking processes. Among them, the prerequisite tree links an objective, its obstacles and the intermediate objectives that overcome them, by necessity. Critical Chain (1997) adds resources to project logic and pools each task's hidden safety into shared buffers.

    Its part hereThe because statements, the council, the chain and its buffer.

  • Last Planner System

    Ballard and Howell (1994)

    Plan in levels, commit only to what can be done, measure promises kept.

    Glenn Ballard and Greg Howell developed the system in 1994 and founded the Lean Construction Institute in 1997; Ballard's 2000 PhD at the University of Birmingham sets it out. Planning runs in levels: master, phase, a six-week lookahead that makes work ready, the weekly plan and a daily huddle. Percent Plan Complete (PPC) is promises kept over promises made, across five conversations: Should, Can, Will, Did, Learn.

    Its part hereHow ten hands keep promises week by week.

  • Critical path method

    Kelley and Walker (1957)

    Earliest and latest times through a network of jobs.

    The critical path method came from James E. Kelley Jr of Remington Rand and Morgan R. Walker of DuPont in 1957; PERT came from the US Navy's Polaris programme in 1958. Kelley (1961) framed the time–cost trade-off as a parametric linear program, and D. R. Fulkerson (1961) solved it as a network flow. Linear programming itself goes back to Leonid Kantorovich (1939) and George Dantzig's simplex method (1947).

    Its part hereFloat, the critical path, the levelled programme.

Backcasting gives the direction, constraints the focus, the critical path the timing and Last Planner the reliability. The sundial adds a shared address: every participant, and anyone's LLM, reads and writes the plan at the same places, not only those standing at the board.

The clock and the blocks

Every job lives at an address on the canonical clock, a ten-digit number that is also a date; the plan lives on the beach as one spine and ten seats written at those addresses.

The rungs

Each digit is a rung of scale, from the beat at pscale 0 to the millennium at pscale 9; the root, pscale 10, holds the project's line.

  • Beat, pscale 0: about 18 minutes.
  • Gathering, pscale 1: 2 hours 40 minutes.
  • Day, pscale 2.
  • Seven-day band, pscale 3: days 1–7, 8–14, 15–21, 22–28 and 29–31.
  • Month, pscale 4.
  • Season, pscale 5: a quarter of the year.
  • Year, decade, century and millennium: pscale 6 to 9.

The buffer

The root-square rule assumes risks are independent; weather and supply delays are not, so the buffer under-protects. Only 1 build in 200 meets the chain's own end, 23 November, and 51.5% overrun the promise. Goldratt's rule of thumb, half the work on the chain, would give 66.2 days instead of 17.6; the simulation says starting about nine working days earlier gives 90% in the model.

The constraint

The council holds about 15 working weeks of the chain: two to validate, eight to determine and five to discharge conditions. Five is the model's assumption; the statutory period for conditions is eight weeks, which would move every start date about three weeks earlier. Theory of Constraints says exploit the constraint first: submit the condition details with the application, which can remove most of that wait.

Rung fit

85% of misfits are jobs of a day or less: short jobs that cross a gathering boundary, and half-day and day jobs that run past 16:00 into the next day. A day and a half of blockwork from Thursday 29 July to Monday 2 August sits at the season node, two rungs above its seven-day band, because it crosses a month end. Letting the scheduler spend up to two days of float lifts fit to 87.7%; declaring Monday-to-Friday weeks barely helps.

Promises

The simulated weekly PPC is 86.9% because the model re-plans perfectly every week. The Lean Construction Institute reports that only 54% of planned work gets done in a typical week; Ballard treats 70% as the level where gains begin.

The last critical run

The kitchen worktop closes the chain: templated once the units are fixed and level, two weeks of fabrication, then fitting.

What the simulation leaves out

A planning refusal, council delays beyond a fifth, no-shows, rework and client changes; it also re-plans perfectly each week. The confidence figures are upper bounds.

Loosening the plan

A house fixes its plan to the day; the same method loosens by raising one dial, the finest rung the spine voices in advance.

The fixity rung

The beach's backcast law voices only the reference and the live path, which is fixity at the top. A construction programme voices every rung down to the day. One number separates them: the finest rung written in advance.

  • A house: fixed to the day. Necessity is physical, durations are known, inspections are mandatory.
  • A maths syllabus: fixed to the term. Necessity is conceptual: fractions before ratio, ratio before proportion.
  • A social venture: fixed to the reference. Only the far aim is fixed; each voice writes its own path.
  • A Lego model: fixed to every step. One builder and one numbered sequence: it fits trivially and tests nothing.

A maths syllabus

A syllabus fixes the term and voices weeks and days as teacher and pupils arrive at them. Its necessity tree is the curriculum's prerequisites, and Theory of Constraints thinking tools are already taught to children.

What the beach still lacks

Last Planner's Can: a make-ready log of constraints cleared ahead of the work. Without it, a mirror can only report BLOCKED after the fact.

Ownership and funding

The plan already says when money and hands are needed; who owns the house is the next calculation, comparing four routes.

When money is needed

A dated need for every resource: design and surveys from December to March, council fees in February and March, deposits for windows, trusses, joists, steel, kitchen and heat pump in late April, and trades from June. Each gate is a natural point to pay for completed stages, and the hours by hand are known.

Four routes

Four ways the house could be owned, to be costed side by side.

  • Conventional investment

    A lender or investor funds land and build, and ownership follows title and shares.

  • Crowdfunding the build

    Many backers fund the whole. In the UK, loan-based and investment-based crowdfunding are regulated by the FCA; donation and reward schemes are not.

  • Shared ownership by contribution

    Hands give hours, suppliers materials and a landholder the plot. Each contribution is recorded at its address, and shares are agreed against the record.

  • Ecosquared

    People direct vector-money at the house's horizons and share it forward. The spine becomes the Projection Map of how far ahead the house is resourced.

Precedents

UK law already supports parts of this. Councils keep self-build and custom housebuilding registers and must permission enough serviced plots to meet them. Community land trusts hold land for a local community and are controlled by their members.

Fulcrum's proposal

Fulcrum's Volume 3 treats money as a vector: directed by people towards future objectives, it makes intentions visible. Volume 2 proposes that money converted into living credits buys conjoint ownership of land and buildings, which turns ownership into stewardship.

In an AI-saturated world

Coordinating many small owners used to cost more than it saved. With each hand's LLM keeping its mirror and anyone able to compute the fold, that cost falls.
