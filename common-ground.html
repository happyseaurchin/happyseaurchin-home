A proposal · 18 November 2026 · the problem beneath the AI churn

Common ground

Your part of the job is done. Now you are waiting on four other firms, each in its own system, and the only way to learn where things stand is to email them and ask. They are emailing you to ask the same.

That is the problem beneath the AI churn. It lives between firms, not inside any one of them, so no firm’s AI can fix it on its own: faster AI in every firm only means faster emails.

    Why now

    Don’t wait for it to arrive from America

    Within a few years every trade will have something that holds the state of its shared work. Either it comes piecemeal, a platform at a time, owned by whoever wins, most likely abroad, and British firms rent it. Or Britain lays the ground now: open like the web, run for its users like Wikipedia, and built on by every firm.

    The second can start today, in every trade at once. It needs ventures funded on the ground, not another platform.

    The whole proposal in one paragraph

    Across Britain, the jobs that matter most are shared by organisations that answer to nobody in common: a home sale, a hospital discharge, a child’s support plan, a pupil’s record, a building’s safety file. Each holds its piece in its own system and the pieces meet by email, so nobody sees where the job stands and somebody spends the day chasing. Every fix on offer has been a platform that needs everyone to join it, and those stall or end up charging for passage. AI will not fix this on its own: the labs undercut each firm’s software every few months, and between the firms AI only speeds up the chasing. What has always lasted is common ground that nobody owns, like the railway gauge, the web and open banking. The beach is that ground for the state of a shared job: each party writes its own part, every party and its AI reads the whole, and anyone can run one. It works today. Fund the ventures that put it to work in every one of these trades now, all at once, and the ground is laid as they go.

    Between two parties only. A bilateral email goes from one firm to one other; nobody else in the job sees it.

    The state of a job

    Where the job stands: who has done what, and what is waiting on whom. A message tells one reader what changed. The state tells every reader where things are.

    Ecosystem problem

    One job carried by many independent organisations, none in charge and some of them rivals, where the trouble lives between them rather than inside any one.

    Common ground

    A shared part that no competitor owns, like a railway gauge or the web’s protocols, on which rivals go on competing.

    Stigmergy

    Working together by leaving marks in a shared place instead of sending messages to one another: how termites build a mound, and how a wiki gets written.

    Selling a home 1.2

    A run of sales held up by twenty firms and their inboxes. When one link fails, every sale in the chain fails with it.

    In England and Wales most homes are sold in a chain: each sale waits on the one below it, and nothing binds until every link exchanges contracts, usually on the same day. Each link brings an estate agent, a conveyancer on each side, usually a broker and a lender with its valuer, often a surveyor, a set of searches, and HM Land Registry behind them. A chain of four sales can mean twenty firms, each in its own system. Nobody can see the whole chain, so agents and conveyancers spend their days ringing and emailing to find out who is waiting on whom.

    120 daysfrom offer accepted to completion, on average: about 60% longer than in 2007
    1 in 3agreed sales falls through, costing movers about £400m a year and the economy up to £1.5bn

    Source: GOV.UK, Home buying and selling reform roadmap, 19 June 2026

    Already common: the Open Property Data Association’s free Property Data Trust Framework, and the government’s 2026 plan for information up front and contracts that bind earlier.

    11 parties to one sale: up to 55 threads between them

    • Many firms, one jobAn agent, two conveyancers, a broker, a lender and its valuer, a surveyor, the searches and the Land Registry, for every sale in the chain.
    • Nobody in chargeNo firm runs the chain. Agents compete for listings, conveyancers for instructions.
    • Moved by messages‘Any news?’ calls and emails, link by link, up and down the chain.
    • Every fix needs everyoneChain trackers and transaction networks each ask every firm in the chain to join theirs.
    • Fixes stall or take a tollHome Information Packs came in 2007 and were suspended in 2010. In Australia one network won almost all digital conveyancing, and a national plan to let a rival work with it was halted.

    Leaving hospital 1.3

    A patient well enough to go home waits on a ring of agencies, each holding one part of the plan.

    When someone is well enough to leave hospital but needs care afterwards, their discharge waits on several bodies at once: the ward and its discharge team, the council’s social care, a home-care agency or a care home with a free room, community nurses, the GP, the NHS body that funds continuing care, and the family. Each keeps its own record. Referrals go by email and form, rooms are found by phone, and the patient waits in a bed while someone chases the parts together.

    13,000people a day in English hospital beds are well enough to leave but still waiting: about one bed in ten
    £10bnspent on the NHS’s national IT programme, begun in 2002 to give the NHS one system and dismantled in 2011

    Source: The King’s Fund, 2026; Nuffield Trust: 13,750 a day in January 2026

    Already common: the NHS number, which the law has asked health and care to use as one consistent identifier since 2015.

    10 parties to one discharge: up to 45 threads between them

    • Many firms, one jobThe ward, the discharge team, council social care, a care agency or care home, community nurses, the GP, the body that funds care, and the family.
    • Nobody in chargeHospitals, councils and private care providers answer to different masters and different budgets.
    • Moved by messagesReferral forms by email, rooms found by phone, and the family ringing everyone.
    • Every fix needs everyoneNational systems that need every party on one platform: the 2002 programme, and the Federated Data Platform contracted to Palantir in 2023.
    • Fixes stall or take a tollThe national programme was dismantled in 2011. Each area keeps its own shared care record, so a patient who crosses a boundary crosses systems.

    A child’s support plan 1.4

    A plan the law says should take twenty weeks, assembled by email from services that each see one part of the child.

    A child in England with special educational needs may need an Education, Health and Care plan. The council must gather advice from the school, an educational psychologist, health professionals such as a paediatrician or speech and language therapist, social care and the family, then write the plan, all within twenty weeks of the request. Each adviser writes in their own system and sends a report as an attachment. The family chases the council, the council chases the advisers, and nobody sees the whole file until the draft arrives.

    46%of new plans were issued within the twenty-week legal limit in 2025
    718,800children and young people had a plan in January 2026, up 12.5% in a year

    Source: Department for Education, Education, health and care plans 2026

    Already common: the plan’s sections, A to K, are set in law and the same in every council.

    8 parties to one plan: up to 28 threads between them

    • Many firms, one jobThe family, the school’s SENCO, the council’s SEN team, an educational psychologist, health professionals and social care.
    • Nobody in chargeSchools, councils and the NHS answer to their own masters, and families and councils can end up on opposite sides at tribunal.
    • Moved by messagesRequests and reports go by email, and the family asks for news.
    • Every fix needs everyoneEach council buys its own case system and portal, and every adviser must use each one they work for.
    • Fixes stall or take a tollThe systems stay local: a family that moves area meets a new council, a new portal and new advisers.

    A pupil’s record 1.5

    Every school runs its records on one vendor’s system, and every app it buys needs its own copy of the same pupils.

    Every school in England keeps its pupils in a management information system bought from one of a few vendors, then buys apps around it: payments, messages home, safeguarding logs, homework, assessment. Each app needs the same pupil data, so brokers sit between the records system and the apps, copying it across. When a pupil moves school, the record is exported from one system and imported into another, and the parts that did not travel are chased by email and phone.

    21,000schools used SIMS, the largest records system, when Capita sold it to private equity in 2020 for up to £400m; it then joined ParentPay Group

    Source: The Register, 14 December 2020

    Already common: the Common Transfer File, the Department for Education’s format for moving a pupil’s record between schools, and the Unique Pupil Number. No vendor owns either.

    9 parties to one move: up to 36 threads between them

    • Many firms, one jobThe school, its records vendor, a dozen app vendors, the data brokers, the council, the Department for Education’s census, and the next school.
    • Nobody in chargeThe vendors compete, and each school is one customer among thousands.
    • Moved by messagesExports, imports and emails between school offices whenever a pupil moves.
    • Every fix needs everyoneEach vendor offers a suite that does everything, if the school takes all of it.
    • Fixes stall or take a tollSuites win schools one at a time and keep them. There is never one ecosystem.

    A building’s safety 1.6

    The law asks for one living record of every high-rise building. The firms that build it each keep their part in their own platform.

    Since the Building Safety Act 2022, new high-rise homes in England need the Building Safety Regulator’s approval before work starts, and a ‘golden thread’ of information that lets those responsible understand the building and keep it safe. A building passes through many hands: the client, architects and engineers, a principal designer, a principal contractor and its subcontractors, product manufacturers, the regulator and the fire service, then the owner who must keep it safe for decades. Each firm keeps its part in its own platform, and the record moves on by export and email.

    22 weeksmedian for the regulator to approve a new high-rise build by summer 2026, down from 43 weeks, against a statutory 12

    Source: GOV.UK, Building Safety Regulator data, June to August 2026

    Already common: the golden thread is a duty in law, not a product, and ISO 19650 is an international standard for managing a building’s information.

    11 parties to one building: up to 55 threads between them

    • Many firms, one jobThe client, architect, engineers, principal designer, principal contractor, subcontractors, manufacturers, the regulator, the fire service, the owner and the residents.
    • Nobody in chargeDesigners and contractors compete for every job, and the owner inherits whatever they hand over.
    • Moved by messagesDrawings and certificates pass by export and email at every stage.
    • Every fix needs everyoneEach main contractor picks its own platform for the project’s documents, and everyone on the job must join it.
    • Fixes stall or take a tollWhen the job ends, the record is exported to the owner, who keeps it in another system. It changes platform at every hand-over.

    1 of 6 1.12

    The job lives between the firms

    Each firm can improve only its own piece. The trouble is in the ground between them.

    One job is carried by many independent organisations, none in charge and some of them rivals. Each holds its piece in its own system, and the pieces meet only through messages.

    When every party must keep every other informed, the channels between them grow with the square of their number: six firms make 15 pairs, twelve make 66, twenty-four make 276. Each pair is a thread of emails somebody must read and answer. A shared ground grows the other way: each new party adds one link.

    The example · a home sale

    Twenty firms on a chain of four sales make up to 190 pairs. The agent sits in most of them, which is why the agent’s day goes on chasing.

    Ten parties round one discharge make up to 45 pairs: the ward rings the council, the council rings the agency, the agency rings the family, and round again.

    Eight parties round one child make up to 28 pairs, and the family is in most of them, usually as the one asking.

    A school with a records system and a dozen apps keeps a dozen copies of every pupil, each kept in step by a broker. Every school in the country does the same.

    Eleven parties on one tower make up to 55 pairs, and the tower outlives most of the firms that built it.

    2 of 6 1.13

    Messages carry news, not the state

    What every party lacks is one thing they can all read: where the job stands.

    A message tells one reader what changed. The state tells every reader where the job stands: who has done what, and what is waiting on whom.

    Email carries messages well and holds no state, so the state of the job exists nowhere whole. It lies in pieces across a dozen inboxes and in the head of whoever chased hardest. To know it, you ask, and everyone asks.

    The example · a home sale

    The buyer’s conveyancer knows the searches are back. The seller’s conveyancer two links up does not, and the agent rings both to learn whether the chain can exchange this week.

    The ward knows the patient is ready, the agency knows it has a carer from Thursday, the council knows the funding is agreed. Nobody holds all three, so the patient stays until someone rings round.

    The psychologist’s report has gone in, the speech therapist’s is late, the school’s is in draft. The family learns which only by asking the council, which learns by asking each.

    When a pupil moves, the new school waits for a file, then emails the old one about the parts that did not travel.

    The fire engineer revised the strategy last month, and a subcontractor is still working from the version before. The difference surfaces at the regulator’s desk.

    The same week of one job, seen two ways. An inbox holds only what was sent to you. The state holds every part, each written by the party that did it.

    3 of 6 1.14

    Chronic, because it is nobody’s to fix

    The gap costs everyone a little, so nobody pays to close it. That is why it lasts.

    This is an ecological problem, not a fault in any one firm. Like a river that every farm along it muddies a little, the cost of the gap is spread across everyone who touches the job, and no single firm gains enough from closing it to pay for that alone.

    Each acts sensibly for itself, and together they stay worse off than a shared arrangement would leave them: economists call it a coordination failure. It is why a house purchase now takes about 60% longer than it did in 2007, and why the same failures turn up review after review.

    The example · a home sale

    A conveyancer who answers chasers faster wins a little time back. The chain still waits on the slowest link, three firms away.

    A delay costs the hospital a bed, the council a stretched care budget, the family weeks of worry: enough for each to complain, and for none to fix it alone.

    A council that hires more caseworkers still waits on advice from services it does not run.

    A school that switches vendor pays for the move and gets a new set of copies. The problem returns with the next app.

    A contractor’s tidy record is exported at handover into the owner’s system, and the care that went into it is lost to the next firm.

    Try it · who gains from closing the gap

    Simple arithmetic, not a measurement: each organisation sits in its own share of the threads, so closing the gap is worth a great deal to them together and only a slice of it to any one of them.

    4 of 6 1.15

    Every fix is a platform

    The usual fix is one firm’s hub. Rivals stay out and it dies, or it wins and charges for passage.

    The fix usually offered is a platform: one company builds a hub and invites everyone onto it. If rivals refuse, the hub dies, or the job splits between hubs that do not speak to each other, and there is never one ecosystem. If it wins, the whole trade depends on one owner, who sets the price of taking part.

    Either way the ground between the firms ends up owned, which is what they would not accept in the first place. TradeLens, the shipping platform Maersk built with IBM, closed in 2023, Maersk saying the industry-wide collaboration it needed had not come: rival carriers had held back from a platform run by a competitor.

    The example · a home sale

    Chain trackers, transaction networks and portals each ask the chain’s firms to join theirs, so one chain can sit across three of them.

    Each area buys its own shared care record, and the national data platform belongs to one company’s contract.

    Each council’s portal asks every adviser to sign in to it, so a psychologist who works for three councils keeps three accounts.

    Each records vendor offers a suite that does everything if the school takes all of it, and the brokers that copy data between suites are paid for the copying.

    Each contractor chooses the project’s document platform. Every firm joins it, and leaves when the job ends.

    5 of 6 1.16

    AI makes it faster, not clearer

    The labs undercut each firm’s software every few months. Between the firms, AI alone only speeds up the chasing.

    AI is arriving in every firm in the shape the work already has: a copilot in the agent’s software, an AI-native conveyancer, a model that drafts the discharge letter. Meanwhile the labs ship, inside their own assistants, much of what specialist products sell. When Anthropic released a legal plugin for its Claude Cowork assistant in early 2026, legal software shares fell by up to a fifth within days; in November it opened a personal finance service inside Claude itself, in the US. What a firm buys this year may be undercut by the next release.

    Between the firms, AIs that can only email each other will send more messages, faster, and the job will stay as blind as it was. Whoever holds the shared state of a trade’s work will hold the trade.

    Sources: Decrypt, February 2026; 9to5Mac, 5 November 2026; the wave itself, trade by trade: the AI churn

    The example · a home sale

    Agents’ software gains copilots and conveyancers go AI-native. The chain’s state is still in twenty inboxes, now answered faster.

    An AI drafts the discharge summary in seconds. The care home still hears about it by phone.

    An AI can draft the plan from the reports in minutes. It still waits on the reports, each from a different service.

    Every vendor adds an assistant to its own suite, and each assistant sees its own vendor’s copy of the pupil.

    An AI checks a design against the regulations. The design still reaches the regulator through a dozen firms’ exports.

    6 of 6 1.17

    What lasted was common ground

    What one company owned failed or took a toll. What nobody owned lasted, and rivals competed on top of it.

    Across nearly two centuries the record is consistent. Where a shared job was handed to one owner’s system, or to one central database, the fix failed, or won and became a toll. Where the shared part belonged to nobody (a gauge, a protocol, a format, a key) it lasted, and the firms went on competing on everything above it: compete on the trains, share the track.

    Britain has done this before. Parliament set one railway gauge in 1846. The web was made by a Briton at CERN and given to the world in 1993. Since 2018 the competition regulator has required the biggest banks to open common interfaces, now used by more than 16 million people and small firms. And in 2026 Parliament legislated for a single identifier for every child in England, for every agency to use, rather than one more database.

    Sources: Open Banking Limited, 2026; Children’s Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026, section 4

    The example · a home sale

    Property already has an open piece: the Property Data Trust Framework, free to every firm. It is a format to exchange, not yet a place where the chain’s state stands.

    Health has its common key: the NHS number, which every party can use and nobody owns.

    The plan’s sections, A to K, are set in law and the same in every council: a common form, ready for a common ground.

    The Common Transfer File and the Unique Pupil Number are the parts of school data no vendor owns, and the parts that move with a pupil.

    The golden thread is a duty in law, not a product, and ISO 19650 is an open standard any firm can meet.

    Step 3 of 5 1.11

    Two ways from here

    In short

    The ground between firms gets laid either way. The question is how soon, by whom, and who owns it.

    In fits and starts 1.112

    • Each trade waits for its own fix, piecemeal: a tracker here, a network there, an AI assistant everywhere.
    • A few networks win and charge for passage; the rest stall and strand their users.
    • The biggest platforms arrive from abroad, already built and funded, and British firms rent the ground.
    • It takes ten years, it is never one ecosystem, and the email goes on underneath.

    Jump, now 1.113

    • Start in every trade at once, now: one venture in each, led by someone who feels the problem in their own work.
    • All of them on one common ground, open like the web and run for its users like Wikipedia.
    • Because the ground is shared, what each venture builds strengthens it for all the others at the same time, and each pays for the ground it stands on.
    • Britain leads the race that comes after the models: not who builds the biggest AI, but where rivals trust the record of their shared work.

    Not by copying the platform race, where big money backs one company’s network until everyone has joined and then charges for passage. The web and Wikipedia took the other road, and they are still standing.

    The first way is what happens if nobody chooses. The second needs one choice, and not a large one: a handful of ventures, funded now and all at once, on ground that already runs. None waits for another; the ground they share is what joins them.

    The example · a home sale

    One estate agency and its conveyancers run a live chain on the ground beside their usual tools, and the next chain joins it.

    One ward and one council’s discharge team keep a patient’s discharge on the ground, each writing its own part.

    One council, its advisers and a group of families keep each plan’s advice in one place, each adviser writing their own part.

    A school and its apps read each pupil’s record from one ground instead of a dozen copies.

    One project keeps its golden thread on the ground from the first drawing, and hands the owner the record itself, not an export.

    Step 4 of 5 1.111

    Our answer, in one thing

    One shared place for each job, where every party writes its own part and every party, and every party’s AI, reads the whole. Nobody owns it. Anyone can run one.

    That place is the beach.1.1112 It is an open-source commons of small numbered blocks, and any AI reaches it through one connector, bsp-mcp, listed on the official MCP Registry. Each party writes its own part under its own latch, and nobody can overwrite another’s. Private parts are sealed by encryption, readable only by those entitled.

    Any organisation can run a beach of its own, so no host owns the ground: it is a protocol, like the web, not a platform. It runs today and is used every day by a small community. It has not yet carried a house chain or a hospital discharge; the first venture in each trade is how it will.

    The example · a home sale

    On the ground, a chain is one place: each link’s agent, conveyancers and lender write their own parts, and anyone in the chain, or their AI, sees at a glance which link is waiting and on what.

    On the ground, a discharge is one place: the ward, the council, the agency and the family each write their own part, and the patient goes home when the last part lands, not when the last call is returned.

    On the ground, a plan is one place: each adviser writes their own advice, the family sees what has arrived and what has not, and the council drafts from the whole.

    On the ground, a pupil’s record is one place: the school writes it once, each app reads what it is allowed to, and a move between schools is a change of who may read it, not an export.

    On the ground, the golden thread is one place: each firm writes its own part as it goes, and the owner inherits the record itself, every part still signed by its writer.
