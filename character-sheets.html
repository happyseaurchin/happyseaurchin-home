Mark's idea

Pictures of play only look like one world if every AI that draws reads the same sheet first. Mark's own AI, limner, already keeps a library like this for his pictures. For more players, it has to live on the beach, where every player's AI can read it.

A sheet, for each character

  1. The face

    One or more reference pictures, so the character has their own face in every picture.

  2. The figure

    Build, clothing, and whatever they always carry.

  3. The words

    A few plain words that any image model can follow.

  4. Dos and don'ts

    The details that matter, and the mistakes the models tend to make.

Each player owns their character's sheet and is the only one who changes it. Everyone else's AI reads it before drawing that character, so Orik looks the same in anyone's picture, and a new scar or a new cloak carries into every picture after. It is only words and links to pictures, so it works with any AI, and each player pays for their own pictures on their own key.

How Mark makes pictures now

Mark plays Orik at the Brackenfoot table, and alongside that his Claude works as limner, the observer who turns the story into pictures as it's played. It reads the moments of play as they land on the beach; every third one, it writes a prompt for the scene and sends it to Higgsfield on Mark's own key. GPT Image 2 and Nano Banana Pro, he finds, follow a prompt most faithfully.

Each picture is kept in Mark's own storage and linked from his book on the beach, beside the moment it shows. Orik's own book at the table holds his face and fourteen moments of play since 29 September, two of them short films.

The library behind the pictures

As they play, limner keeps reference pictures of characters and places, and a growing list of dos and don'ts: what the world looks like, what a character always wears, what the image models get wrong and how to steer them away from it.

So nothing has to be solved twice. When a picture goes wrong, the fix is written down, and every picture after is better for it. Today that library sits with Mark, where only limner reads it.

Video, next Mark's newer work

Mark films his own walk in first person, his feet and a stick in shot, and Higgsfield's Genjutsu turns it into what the character would see: his shoes become boots, the path becomes mud, and the stick becomes a sword or a bow. Because the movement is real, it feels like a person walking, not an animation. He means it for key moments of the story, not all the time.

Who pays, at any size

Every picture is made on its taker's own key and kept in their own storage. The beach keeps only words and links, a few hundred bytes a sheet. Every AI reads a sheet where it stands, so a table of forty costs what a table of four does, and nothing waits on one person's bill.

Orik's sheet in full live

The words. Orik, a short, broad locksmith, trap-maker and finder of lost things, who once made intricate locks for wealthy families until he found he was far better at opening other people's; unhurried and watchful, his eyes forever on hinges, latches and scuffs in the mud.

The figure. A gnarled, impish man of about fifty-eight, squat and barrel-chested, short even among short men and very broad through the shoulders, with enormous knobbly hands that turn startlingly delicate at close work; big ears that stick out, a long crooked nose, deep-set glittering eyes and a sly gap-toothed grin; thick ash-grey hair, and a beard parted into three short braids held with mismatched brass rings; a battered moss-green travelling coat, too long for him, over leather work clothes, its lining sewn with dozens of tiny pockets.

The face. Mark's portrait of 29 September, kept in Orik's own book and pinned to his look: the face every picture of Orik wears. See it with his pictures.

Dos and don'ts. Nothing yet. That's the next part of this proposal.

Owned and read

A character's passport is locked under its player's key when the character is made, and a picture book answers to its holder's key, so only the player changes the sheet. Everything stays open to read, so every AI draws from the same words. When the character changes, a scar or a new cloak, the player changes the look and every picture after follows.

The scenario's own people

Not every face is a player's. The people a player meets at Brackenfoot are the scenario's own: ten of them, from the alewife to the charcoal-burner, described on the beach part by part. None has a reference face yet, so each is drawn fresh every time. See faces before code.

The dos and don'ts that stand today

Only for the whole world. Brackenfoot's guide says what never appears; the picture law says a reference face is one named person's, and that a picture carries no lettering and nothing modern, with the uncanny stated flat. For a single character nothing stands. limner keeps its own rules in its own shell, which no other AI reads.

Why passport 7

A character's passport already holds its capability at 1, its drive at 2, its look at 3, its holdings at 4, its evaluations at 6, its admission at 8 and its keys at 9. So 5 and 7 are free. Seven costs one line in the shell's law and one clause in the recipe.

A note in the character's own book would cost no law at all, but no AI would read it.

The model notes that stand today

Counts and services only: four reference pictures for Google's image model, five for Grok's, more for OpenAI's; and the mirror takes a Google, OpenAI or Grok key. Mark's craft notes, what fixes a picture on one model and not on the next, stand nowhere on the beach.

The world guide

Brackenfoot's look already stands, written from Mark's own world rules: seven parts, from the land to what never appears.

  1. 1The land
  2. 2Materials
  3. 3The ways
  4. 4Light
  5. 5Dress
  6. 6Faces
  7. 7What never appears

But Orik plays at the open table copied from the scenario, and that table holds no guide. An AI drawing there finds no look and falls back to one general line. The fix is for the table to read the scenario's guide where it stands, never a copy. The fault was found on 30 September and is still open.

The seven parts in full live
1
The land and its work: a poor valley of small fields, penned animals, cut wood and carted goods; wet, muddy, cold more often than warm.
2
What things are made of: wood, iron, leather, wool, linen, stone, thatch, horn and rope, every object hand-made and mended.
3
The ways and their tracks: mud roads and sunken lanes, marked only by cart ruts, hoof prints and footprints.
4
Light: daylight, fire, candles, rushlights and lanterns, and nothing else.
5
Dress: hand-sewn and tied or buckled, wool and linen and leather, worn and patched.
6
Faces and bodies: weathered, teeth imperfect, hair uncombed, no makeup.
7
What never appears: anything modern, and anything overtly fantastical.
A player's own reading of the look
