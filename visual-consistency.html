So when a moment is drawn, the pictures of everything in it can be found by those addresses. Each address has someone with the say over how it looks, and their pictures lead. Most of this already stands. What's missing is the say itself, the weight, pictures of the places themselves, the set, and one gathering for everyone who draws.

Two stories, one index

The story in words and the story in pictures are kept apart, and they meet at the moment. Words lead for what happens and who someone is: the room's record holds what happened, each character's own account holds their story, the world's maps hold what lasts, and every telling is composed from them. Pictures lead for how things look.

Both are found by the same three things: where, when and who. A picture never changes the record, and a telling never takes its facts from a picture. A lasting change, a scar or a new cloak, passes from the record into the look by its holder's hand: the words first, then a new picture. Until then the picture follows the telling, which is why Orik's cut calf at the Diggings shows only where a telling says so.

What works today

Every picture is a link in its maker's book, its file on the maker's own storage: Mark's on his own, David's on the beach's commons store. A face is found by its address across every book at the table, so Orik's face holds from picture to picture, and Ugarth's face rides in Mark's pictures as well as David's.

A place comes with its own words from the world's map, the people of a place come from their register part by part, and the world's look comes in its seven parts. Since 23 October the open table's pictures page reads that look whole. The newest picture of a room rides as a reference, so David's two pictures in the Long House keep one room. One recipe for every picture stands in the beach's picture law.

What's missing or wrong
  1. Nobody can hand the say over a look to someone else. A character's own book leads for their face, and nothing leads for a place or a person of the place.
  2. The newest picture always leads, so a stray one becomes the reference for the next. A fight at the Diggings is now the Diggings' reference.
  3. Places have no pictures of their own, only pictures of moments that happened there. Mark has made two without being asked: the taproom, and Orik on the slopes.
  4. Brackenfoot's ten people have no faces.
  5. A character has one face and no views.
  6. Four seats gather references four ways (the pictures page, the mirror, limner and a script), and they drift apart.
  7. Mark's store won't let a web page read his pictures, so none of them can be a reference in a browser. He has been told.
  8. Nothing records what a picture was drawn from, so drift can't be traced.
  9. When a place's own words and the world's look disagree, nothing says which holds.
Who pays, at any scale

Each picture is made on its taker's own key and kept on their own storage; the beach keeps only words and links. The say also narrows what has to be read: a seat reads the world's book, the books of the people in frame and the books they point to, never every book at the beach. So a table of forty costs what a table of four does.

Where the line goes

For a character, at the passport's 7, a free position that Mark's character sheets already propose for the character's dos and don'ts. Its first line names who has the say over the character's pictures and points at their book; the dos and don'ts stand beneath it.

For a world, in its own book, gallery:brackenfoot at the scenario's own surface. The picture law already names a world's own book for the pictures its author adopts, and it has never been made. Whoever has the say keeps it, and it holds only the pictures that lead. Every table forked from Brackenfoot reads it there, as it reads the world's look and its people.

What the say can't do

It never overrules the world: what never appears in a world never appears in any picture of it, whoever pictures a character. It never writes anyone else's block: a player's words for their character stay the player's, and whoever has the say keeps their pictures in their own book. It never locks anyone out of drawing: an observer, a watcher or any AI draws from the same pictures, keeps what they make in their own book, and leads only where the say names them. And the holder takes the say back with the same one line.

The weight of a reference

Which picture leads is settled by the say and by scale, never by a count. Each step down this ladder is taken only when the one above has nothing to give.

  1. The pictures of whoever has the say over that very thing, newest first.
  2. Their pictures of the place above it: a room's building, a building's region.
  3. The last picture of the same scene, so what just happened carries over.
  4. Anyone else's, newest first. Useful, but never above the say.

Words follow the same rule: the finer holds. The moment's telling holds over the place's own words, and the place's words hold over the world's look.

Why never a count

Counting how often a picture is used would make whatever is drawn most the most important, so a mistake repeated ten times would become the look. The say is a person's judgement, kept where anyone can read it, and changed with one line.

A reference carries everything in it two of Mark's pictures

Whatever is in a leading picture is copied into every picture after it, faults and all.

So a picture is checked against the words and the world's look before it leads.
