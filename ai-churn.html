The AI churn

AI is churning the software every trade buys. Beneath the churn lies the ground between firms, where one job is shared by many and nobody is in charge, and several networks are racing to own it.

Walk through it Give feedback Ask in the room
The whole explainer in one paragraph

The AI labs now ship, every few months and inside their own assistants, what used to be a specialist product, so what a trade buys this year may be undercut the next. Beneath the churn lies an older problem that no product has solved: one job shared by many firms that compete, where nobody can be in charge and the work moves by email. With the English house chain as its worked case, this explainer sets out that problem whole, finds it in other trades, shows why every platform built to fix it has failed or become a toll, and what kind of common ground has lasted.

Step 1 of 61

The ecosystem problem

Some jobs belong to no one firm. The trouble lives between the firms, so no firm can fix it alone.

15 pairswhen 6 firms must keep each other informed
276 pairswhen 24 firms must
The step in full

Some jobs belong to no single organisation. A house sale, a hospital discharge, a container crossing an ocean: each is one job carried by many independent firms, none of which can be in charge, and many of which are rivals. Each firm holds its own piece of the job in its own system, and the pieces meet only through messages, emails, calls and forms typed again at every hand-over. Nobody sees the whole, so somebody spends their day chasing, and the job moves at the pace of its slowest reply. The failure lives in the ground between the firms, not inside any one of them, so no firm can fix it by improving itself, and every fix that asks all the firms to join one firm's system meets rivals who will not.

Five signs1.2

An ecosystem problem shows five signs together. Many independent firms each hold a piece of one job. None of them can be in charge, and many are rivals. The job moves by messages, and somebody spends their day chasing. Every fix on offer is a platform that works only if everyone joins it. And those fixes either die because rivals will not join, or win and become a toll everyone must pay. Any one sign alone is ordinary business; all five together mark a trade whose problem lives between its firms.

Messages grow with the square1.3

When every party must keep every other informed, the one-to-one channels grow with the square of the number of parties: 6 firms make 15 pairs, 12 make 66, 24 make 276. Each pair is a thread of emails somebody must read and answer. A shared ground grows the other way: each new party adds one link, to the ground. This is arithmetic, not opinion, and it is why adding people, or faster tools, to a chain of messages makes it louder rather than quicker.

State, not messages1.4

What the parties lack is not more messages but one thing they can all read: the state of the job, who has done what and what is waiting on whom. A message tells one reader what changed; the state tells every reader where things stand. Email carries messages well and holds no state, which is why the firms that share a job live in their inboxes.

Why no firm can fix it1.5

Each firm can improve only its own piece. A faster solicitor still waits on the slowest survey; a better system still re-types what another firm's system already holds. The cost of the gap falls a little on everyone and on nobody enough to pay for closing it. Economists call this a coordination failure: each party acting sensibly for itself leaves all of them worse off than a shared arrangement would.

The platform trap1.6

The usual fix is a platform: one firm builds a hub and invites everyone onto it. If its rivals refuse, the hub dies, or the job splits between rival hubs that do not speak to each other, so there is never one ecosystem. If it wins, the whole trade depends on one owner, who can set the price of taking part in the job. Either way the ground between the firms ends up owned, which is what the firms would not accept in the first place.

Step 2 of 61.1

The house chain

A house sale is a run of dominoes held up by some twenty firms and their emails. One falls, and the whole run falls.

104 daysfrom offer to exchange, on average, in April 2026
26 to 37%of agreed sales never completed in 2025, depending on who counts
The step in full

In England and Wales most home sales hang in a chain: a first-time buyer buys from someone who is buying from someone else, on up to a seller who buys nothing. Every link is a transaction of its own, with an estate agent, a conveyancer for each side, usually a lender and a broker, often a surveyor, searches, and HM Land Registry behind them, so a chain of four sales can involve twenty firms or more, each in its own system. Nothing binds until contracts are exchanged, and in practice every link exchanges on the same day and completes on the same day, so the chain moves at the pace of its slowest link and collapses when any one link fails. The firms keep track of each other by email and phone, and none of them can see the whole chain.

Who is in one link1.12

The seller and the buyer; the seller's estate agent; a conveyancer or solicitor for each side; the buyer's mortgage broker and lender, and the lender's valuer; often a surveyor the buyer chooses; the local authority and other searches; and HM Land Registry, which records the new owner. The agent, both conveyancers and the lender each check identities for money laundering separately. Then the next link up the chain has its own set again, sharing only the household that sits in both.

How long, and how often it fails1.13

In Great Britain the average home that exchanged contracts in April 2026 had gone under offer 104 days earlier, against 76 days in 2019. Between a quarter and more than a third of agreed sales never complete, depending on who counts: Quick Move Now put it at 26% for 2025, and Connells at 37%. In the second quarter of 2026, 27% of the sales that failed collapsed at the survey stage.

Sourcesconnellsgroup.co.uktheintermediary.co.uknewsfilecorp.com

The dominoes1.14

A chain completes like a row of dominoes: on completion day the money from the first buyer's lender lets the first seller buy, whose purchase lets the next seller buy, on up the chain. Before that day, a chain holds only if every link holds: if each link had a nine-in-ten chance, a chain of five would hold about six times in ten. Every link's delay is everyone's delay, and one link's failure ends every sale in the chain.

What has been tried1.15

Home Information Packs, required of sellers from 2007, were suspended in 2010. HM Land Registry's Chain Matrix, a web board to show how far each chain had got, was shelved in 2007 with its wider plans for electronic conveyancing, after millions spent with IBM. Private chain trackers and portals have multiplied since, each asking the chain's firms to join it. The government's reform, proposed in October 2025 and extended in June 2026, would require a sales pack with searches and a condition report before a home is listed, then contracts that bind earlier once the packs are in place, with the laws coming over this Parliament; the Open Property Data Association's free, open framework for property data added milestones every party in a chain can share in its 2025 version.

Step 3 of 61.11

The churn

AI arrives in each firm's own piece. The labs undercut the software in the middle, and the chain's shared state is up for grabs.

18%Thomson Reuters' fall after the AI legal plugins of early 2026
99%of digital conveyances in most Australian states run by one network
The step in full

AI is arriving in every firm of the chain, and it arrives in the shape the chain already has. Each firm buys AI for its own piece: an agent's software gains a copilot, a conveyancer goes AI-native, a chain tracker flags risk. Meanwhile the AI labs ship, inside their own assistants, much of what those specialist products sell, so a tool bought this year may be undercut by the next release. What the labs cannot undercut is a solicitor's licence or the Land Registry's register. What is still up for grabs is the shared state of the chain, who has done what and what each sale waits on, and several networks are racing to own it. If one wins, the ground between the firms is owned; if none does, the firms' AIs send each other more messages, faster, and the chain stays as blind as it was.

The labs move in1.112

Anthropic released plugins for its Claude Cowork assistant on 30 January 2026, a legal one among them, and in the days after, legal and software shares fell hard: Thomson Reuters by 18% and LegalZoom by nearly 20%, on the fear that firms will pay for fewer seats of specialist software. On 5 November 2026 Anthropic launched Personal Finance in Claude, a read-only US beta in which people link bank and card accounts through Plaid and ask about spending and bills, with no ads and no products of its own; Anthropic says it is exploring how Claude could work with licensed professionals and regulated firms. OpenAI added similar tools to ChatGPT in May 2026. What the labs shipped through 2026, and how fast each specialist head start shrank, stands at spine:waterline 1.2 and 1.5.

Sourceslawnext.comdecrypt.co9to5mac.com

AI in each firm1.113

Farringdon opened in London in April 2026 as an AI-native conveyancer, its AI agents doing most of the work of each transaction; Conveyd raised £2.5m for an AI conveyancing platform; Reapit, whose software many estate agents run on, announced a voice-led AI copilot; XchangeHero sells AI chain tracking to independent agents. Small firms lag: of four firms on one English high street asked in November 2026, two used no AI at all.

Sourcesmortgagesolutions.co.ukcfotech.co.ukpulse.botthenegotiator.co.uk

The race for the chain's state1.114

Several networks now offer to hold the state of the chain, each asking every firm to join it. LMS runs a shared data network for property transactions. PEXA, which runs almost every electronic conveyance in Australia, went live in the UK with NatWest for remortgages in March 2026 and has added sales and purchases. Chain trackers sell to agents one firm at a time. Each is a sound business; together they rebuild the problem, a chain split across networks that do not share.

Sourcescorporate.lms.comifamagazine.com

Australia, where one network won1.115

Australia's states made electronic conveyancing compulsory while PEXA was the only operator. A parliamentary committee found PEXA carrying about 99% of digital transactions in most states, and the national scheme meant to let a rival operator work with it was halted. When one network wins, a trade does not get one ecosystem; it gets one owner, and then spends years trying to make room for a second.

Sourcesaph.gov.auitnews.com.au

The open current1.116

The connections AI agents use are going the other way. MCP, through which an AI reaches other tools, and A2A, through which agents reach each other, now sit with the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation, so no single lab owns them. In property, the Open Property Data Association's framework is free to every firm, and it works with government through the Digital Property Market Steering Group. Open rails for agents and open formats for data are not yet a shared place where the state of a job stands, but they are what such a place would be built on.

Sourcesitbrief.co.ukpropertyindustryeye.com

What the labs cannot take1.117

A lab can ship the software in the middle. It cannot hold a solicitor's practising certificate or keep the land register, both protected by law, and it is not the party rivals trust to keep the record of their shared job. The open question of this explainer follows: whoever holds the shared state of a trade's work holds the trade.

Step 4 of 61.111

Other trades

The same five signs, in four shapes, from hospital wards to shipping lanes. Tap a shape to open its cases.

4 shapeschains, rings, relays and echoes
10 caseseach with what was tried, and what became of it
The step in full

The house chain is one case of a pattern that runs through public services and private trade alike. Wherever one job is shared by organisations that answer to no one in common, the five signs appear together. The shapes differ: in a chain each step waits on the last; in a ring many agencies surround one person; in a relay one record passes between rival systems; in an echo every party repeats the same check. The clearest cases follow, each with what was tried.

Chains1.1112

Each step waits on the one before, and one failure stops everything behind it.

Hospital discharge1.11121

A patient well enough to leave waits on an assessment, a care package, a care home place and a funding decision, each held by a different body: the hospital, the council, a care provider, the NHS. On any day thousands of hospital beds in England hold people who are medically ready to go. The national NHS IT programme, begun in 2002 to give the NHS one system, was dismantled in 2011 after costing about £10bn; the latest national answer is the Federated Data Platform, contracted to Palantir in 2023, while each area keeps its own shared care record.

Shipping papers1.11122

A container's documents, the bill of lading, invoices, certificates and customs forms, pass between shipper, carrier, ports, customs, banks and insurers, much of it still on paper or by email. TradeLens, built by Maersk with IBM and launched in 2018, closed in 2023, Maersk saying the full industry collaboration it needed had not been achieved, as rival carriers held back from a platform run by one of their largest competitors; the banks' own trade networks, we.trade, Marco Polo and Contour, folded between 2022 and 2023. The UK's Electronic Trade Documents Act 2023 took another road: it made electronic trade documents legally valid on any system that meets its tests, a rule rather than a platform.

Rings1.1113

Many agencies surround one person, each seeing one slice.

Children's safeguarding1.11131

Review after review of a child's death, from Victoria Climbié's in 2000 onwards, finds that police, health, schools and social care each held a piece and nobody held the whole. ContactPoint, a national database of every child in England, cost about £224m and was switched off in 2010. The Children's Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026 answers with a duty on agencies to share what they know and a single identifier for each child, now being piloted: a common key rather than a platform.

Sourcessacpa.org.uk

Several disadvantages at once1.11132

People facing addiction, homelessness, offending and poor mental health together meet a separate service for each, every one assessing them again and seeing one slice of the same life. Coalitions such as Making Every Adult Matter have worked for years to join these services up area by area, and each area keeps its own systems, so a person who moves starts again. This is the ground the LERO work on this beach stands on (spine:leros-on-the-commons).

Relays1.1114

One record passes between rival systems and is typed again at every hand-over.

School records1.11141

Every school in England keeps its pupils in a management information system bought from one of a handful of vendors. SIMS, long the largest, was sold by Capita to private equity in 2020 for up to £400m and later joined ParentPay Group. What crosses every vendor is the Common Transfer File, the Department for Education's shared format for moving a pupil's record to a new school: the one piece none of them owns.

Sourcestheregister.com

Smart meters1.11142

Britain's first smart meters often stopped working as smart meters when a household switched supplier, because each supplier's system was its own. The fix was a shared national network run by the Data Communications Company under licence, and years of moving millions of meters onto it.

Music royalties1.11143

No shared record says who owns which song across the world, so royalties leak between the collecting societies and publishers that pay writers. The Global Repertoire Database, meant to be that record, was abandoned in 2014 after years of work, when the societies could not agree how to fund and run it.

Echoes1.1115

Every party runs the same check, or asks the same questions, again.

Identity checks1.11151

In one house sale the estate agent, both conveyancers and the lender each check the same person's identity for money laundering, separately, and the next sale up the chain checks everyone again. GOV.UK Verify, the government's attempt at one identity for public services, closed in 2023; the UK now certifies identity providers against a published trust framework, a rulebook many firms can meet rather than one system everyone must join.

Grant applications1.11152

A small charity tells the same story in a different form for every funder it applies to. 360Giving's open standard lets funders publish what they gave in one shared format, but applications are still one form per funder.

Supplier questionnaires1.11153

Every large buyer sends its suppliers its own questionnaire on carbon and conduct, so a supplier fills in dozens, and rival rating platforms each ask suppliers to join them. The Partnership for Carbon Transparency, convened by the World Business Council for Sustainable Development, publishes an open way to exchange product carbon footprints instead.

The pattern in the cases1.1116

In every case above, the fixes that asked everyone to depend on one owner's system or one central database failed or became a toll, and the pieces that lasted belonged to none of the parties: a format, the Common Transfer File; a key, a child's single identifier; a rule, the Electronic Trade Documents Act; a shared network under licence, the smart meters' DCC; a rulebook, the identity trust framework. Step 1.1111 sets the two kinds side by side.

Step 5 of 61.1111

What lasts

What one party owned failed or became a toll. What nobody owned lasted. Compete on the trains, share the track.

1846Parliament sets one railway gauge for new passenger lines
6 and 8owned fixes that failed, common grounds that lasted
The step in full

Across nearly two centuries the record is consistent. Where a shared job was handed to one owner's system, or to one central database, the fix failed, or it won and became a toll. Where the shared part belonged to no competitor, as a gauge, a protocol, a format or a key, it lasted, and the firms went on competing on everything above it: compete on the trains, share the track. Such ground almost never builds itself, because nobody can charge for it. It is a public good, investors fund what can be owned, and accelerators fund apps, not ecosystems; what lasted was paid for in other ways.

The gauge1.11112

In the 1840s rival British railway companies laid track to different widths. Where a broad-gauge line met a standard one, as at Gloucester, every passenger changed trains and every load was carried across by hand. The Gauge Act of 1846 required new passenger lines to be built to one standard gauge, and the companies went on competing on routes, fares and trains, on track all of them could use. The break of gauge is what happens to information at every hand-over between firms today.

What failed1.11113

Each of these asked every party to depend on one owner or one centre, and met parties who would not or could not: HM Land Registry's Chain Matrix, shelved in 2007; ContactPoint, switched off in 2010; the NHS national IT programme, dismantled in 2011; the Global Repertoire Database, abandoned in 2014; GOV.UK Verify, closed in 2023; TradeLens, closed in 2023. Where one network did win, as PEXA in Australia, the trade gained an owner rather than an ecosystem.

What lasted1.11114

Standard gauge, required in 1846; email, on open standards since the 1970s; the web, given away by CERN in 1993; DOIs for research papers, run since 2000 by the non-profit Crossref, and ORCID for researchers, since 2012, used by rival publishers alike; open banking, since 2018, when the competition regulator required the nine largest banks to open common interfaces, now used by more than ten million people; the Electronic Trade Documents Act 2023, and a single identifier for each child in 2026. Each belonged to no competitor, and each let competitors go on competing.

Who paid for what lasted1.11115

Four ways. Required: a law or a regulator made the parties build it, fund it or meet it, as Parliament did for the gauge and the competition regulator for open banking; the laws of 2023 and 2026 for electronic trade documents and a single identifier for each child ask no one to buy a platform, only to meet a common test or use a common key. Shared: the members who use it pay together, as publishers and universities fund Crossref and ORCID through their membership fees. Given: a public body built it and gave it away, as CERN did the web. Carried: it rides on what people already pay for, as email and the web ride on the networks and hosting people buy. Funding for open infrastructure is thin even so: NLnet's NGI Zero, the European fund built for it, took its last applications on 1 June 2026 and has paused while the programme is reviewed.

Step 6 of 61.11111

What an answer needs

Seven tests for a ground that lasts. Nothing yet passes all seven, and each trade's answer will come from people inside it.

7 testsowned by none, state, own part, any AI, cheap and anywhere, private, proven
0candidates that pass all seven today
The step in full

The churn makes the choice urgent, because the firms' AIs are being wired up now, and whatever they are wired to becomes the ground between the firms. An answer that lasts would pass seven tests: owned by no competitor; holding the state of the job, not only messages about it; letting each party write its own part and nobody else's; readable and writable by whichever AI each firm uses; cheap enough for the smallest firm and able to run anywhere, so that no host becomes the toll; private where it must be; and proven across a whole trade. Nothing yet passes all seven. The answer for each trade will come from people inside it, each in their own context, which is why every step here asks for their feedback.

The seven tests1.111112

Owned by none: no competitor holds the ground, or can close it. State: every party reads where the job stands, not only what changed. Own part: each party writes its own part, and nobody can overwrite another's. Any AI: whichever lab or tool a firm uses can read and write it. Cheap and anywhere: the smallest firm can afford it, and it runs on any host, so no host becomes the toll. Private where it must be: sealed parts are readable only by those entitled. Proven: a whole trade uses it.

How the candidates fare1.111113

Read against the seven tests in order. Email: owned by none; holds no state; each writes its own; any AI; cheap and anywhere; private only in part; proven. It fails on state, which is the whole problem. A platform: owned; holds the state; each writes its own only as the owner allows; any AI only where the owner opens it; neither cheap nor anywhere; private; proven only where it has won. A lab's own agent: owned; holds the state in one vendor's memory; each writes its own only in part; best with that vendor's AI; cheap, but only on the vendor's own host; private only in part; not yet proven. An open data standard, such as the property data framework: owned by none; a format to exchange rather than a place where the state stands; each writes its own only in part; any AI; cheap and anywhere; private only in part; its adoption not yet measured. This reading is the explainer's own and open to challenge at this step's point.

One attempt: the beach1.111114

The pscale beach, built by the people behind this explainer, is an open-source commons of small numbered blocks that any AI can read and write through one connector, bsp-mcp, on the official MCP Registry. Each party writes its own part under its own latch and nobody can overwrite another's; any organisation can run a beach of its own, so no host owns the ground; privacy is a separate seal, by encryption. It is live and used daily by a small community, early, unmeasured in any trade, and has not yet met a house chain. It is named here as one attempt at the first six tests, not as the answer: it has not passed the seventh.

Players in their own trade1.111115

No outsider can design a trade's common ground. What a trade needs is one person inside it who feels the problem in their own work, as the estate agency manager did, and a venture they lead in their own context, funded as ventures are, whose use pays for the common ground it stands on. Each such venture leaves the ground readier for the next trade.
