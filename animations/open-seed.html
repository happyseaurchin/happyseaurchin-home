Reading it

  • Width is modelled weight, not market share or money. A record's level (how fundamental it is) sets its full width; an adoption curve grows it from a point at its origin, and decline thins it. A strand is as thick as the records it holds.
  • Colour is heat: bright where innovation is happening, grey once it has become ground everything else stands on.
  • Nesting is lineage: a technology grows inside the field it opened from, and can swell until it fills it.
  • Threads are fusions: other ingredients that came together at the origin.
  • Dashed rings are claims: significance asserted for a young technology, drawn apart from what it has shown so far.
  • Pulses are arrivals, taller for more fundamental ones. Clusters are the punctuations.
  • Gold coins are money where it touched a strand: solid where it changed hands, open where it was valued or lost, with gold arcs where it moved between strands. Coin size is on a log scale, so small sums stay visible beside huge ones; hover a coin for its amount.

    How it was commercialised

    1969 set both paths going. IBM began pricing software separately from its machines in the same year that Unix, ARPANET and the first Request for Comments appeared. The Business strand traces how sharing was then made to pay, and the gold coins show where money actually touched each strand.

    • Sell the support. Cygnus (1989), then Red Hat, the first open-source company to earn $1bn in a year (2012).
    • Sell a second licence, or the extras. MySQL's dual licence (2000) and the open-core firms that followed.
    • Give away the layer beneath your business. Google's Android (2007) and Meta's Llama (2023) make a rival's product free.
    • Rent it out. Cloud providers sell open-source databases and search as services, and keep much of the revenue.
    • Fund it together. Foundations such as Apache (1999) and Linux (2007) hold shared code that competitors pay to maintain.
    • Buy it. Microsoft bought GitHub ($7.5bn, 2018) and IBM bought Red Hat ($34bn, 2019).

    Why firms hesitate

    • Copyleft licences can oblige a firm to publish its own changes.
    • Shared research can give away an advantage that internal R&D would have kept.
    • Dependencies can be attacked: the xz backdoor (2024) was planted by a patient fake maintainer.
    • No single vendor answers for the code.
    • Released model weights cannot be recalled, so any misuse risk is permanent.

    Why they use it anyway

    • It is cheaper than building every layer alone, by a factor of about three and a half.
    • Shared layers become standards that customers and recruits already know.
    • Foundations and security funds now share the cost of maintenance.
    • Giving one layer away can protect the layer you sell.
    • Open weights let outsiders inspect, test and adapt models, and run them on their own machines.

    From an operating system to an open system

    Linux became the shared layer under the world's servers. The proposal behind pscale and the beach is a shared layer for LLMs. Set side by side, the conditions that made Linux a standard read as a checklist for that proposal.

    Earlier "LLM OS" ideas, such as Andrej Karpathy's (2023) and MemGPT (2023), treat a model as the kernel of one system. The open-system proposal standardises what lies between models. In that respect it resembles the Web as much as Linux: anyone can host a beach, and its addresses work across hosts.
