The million that went round

Cash changes hands; things keep the last price paid for them. Run your coin story through and one million in cash ends up counted as five.

Ballpark: who has walked this ground
  • David Graeber, Debt: The First 5,000 Years (2011). Credit came first; coins and markets later.
  • Brahmagupta (628) and Luca Pacioli (1494). Debts as negative numbers; every sum written twice.
  • Henry George, Progress and Poverty (1879). Land rises with the town around it, not with its owner’s work.
  • Hyman Minsky. A boom tips into “Ponzi finance” when debts can be met only if prices keep rising.
  • Eric Williams, Capitalism and Slavery (1944). The triangle’s profits helped finance Britain’s industrial rise; historians still argue how much.
  • Bank of England, Money creation in the modern economy (2014). Loans create deposits.
  • Josh Ryan-Collins, Toby Lloyd and Laurie Macfarlane, Rethinking the Economics of Land and Housing (2017). Mortgage credit and house prices feed each other.
  • Thomas Piketty, Capital in the Twenty-First Century (2013). Wealth outgrows income when returns outrun growth.

        A loop only books revenue

        Both triangles have three legs, each one a sale, and a hub that books profit on every leg. A closed loop cannot make value, and its costs leak out on every turn. What keeps either turning is what comes in from outside, and whether it is paid for.

        The test on 19 November. The loop met the public. Anthropic priced its shares at $83, above the $70 to $78 it had set, opened at $111.50, raised about $81bn plus $26bn placed privately, and touched a market value of $2.1tn, the largest listing on record. That proves appetite for the shares at that price, on that day. It does not yet prove that customers’ payments will grow into the marks, which is the test that matters. Part of the outside money came from inside the loop: Nvidia bought $10bn of shares tied to the listing. The wider market fell the same afternoon.

        When the input stops

        Abolition, in 1833. The owners were paid £20m for their “property”, the people freed got nothing, and the borrowing was finally redeemed in 2015.

        The loop drains: costs keep leaking while booked revenue shrinks. Critics point to telecom vendor financing around 2000 and the savings-and-loan “daisy chains” of the 1980s. Nvidia answers that the firms it backs earn mostly from outside customers.

        Where it breaks

        In kind, completely: one triangle’s input was human beings. The rhyme is in the geometry, a loop fed by something it does not pay for in full, and not in the harm.

        For any loop, ask what comes in from outside, and whether it is paid.
