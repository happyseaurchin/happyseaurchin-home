Where the ends go

One cylinder, five completions

Take 0 out of the line and two open half-lines remain. Take 0 out of the plane and what remains is, in logarithmic coordinates, an infinite cylinder. Every stage of the animation is that same space with its ends filled in differently.

Michael Shulman gives the same menu for an infinite cylinder. Adding a single point at infinity gives a pinched torus, adding a circle at infinity gives an ordinary torus, adding a circle another way gives a Klein bottle, and adding one point in each direction gives a sphere.

Which way round

The convention is exact. Angles are measured anticlockwise, so eiθ with θ > 0 turns anticlockwise, and a contour run anticlockwise is called positively oriented. On the closed circle, with 0 at the bottom, the number x sits at the angle α = 2 arctan x. That is an odd function, so the sign of x is the sign of the turn. As x → +∞ the turn tends to +π, as x → −∞ it tends to −π, and the two ends meet because eiπ = e−iπ.

After the pinch the half-turn becomes a full turn, since 0 and ∞ are now one point. The two lobes are ½(1 + eiψ) and −½(1 + e−iψ) with ψ = 2 arctan(ln|x|), the same rotation and its conjugate.

Clockwise depends on the side you look from. In the plane every meridian circulates the same way: up through the unit circle on the outside, over the top, and down through the seam on the inside, the circulation of a smoke ring. The opposite spins of the 1D picture are that one circulation cut by a plane and seen on the two sides of the axis. Multiplying by eiπ turns the torus half-way round its axis and carries the +1 circle onto the −1 circle. Multiplying by eiθ moves points the long way round the torus, scaling by et moves them the short way, and z ↦ et + iθz does both, tracing a helix on the torus and a straight line on the cylinder.

Positive zero meets positive infinity

The reciprocal already pairs them. As x → 0⁺, 1/x → +∞, and as x → 0⁻, 1/x → −∞. Floating-point arithmetic keeps the sign of zero for this reason, so that 1/(+0) = +∞ and 1/(−0) = −∞.

On each separated circle, x ↦ 1/x is the mirror flip across the line through ±1 and the seam. The seam is the reciprocal's second fixed point, opposite the unit.

Splitting a point into its directions of approach is called a real oriented blow-up. On the line, 0 becomes 0⁺ and 0⁻. In the plane, 0 becomes a whole circle of directions, and so does ∞. The ring torus glues those two circles together, direction to direction.

Cutting the seam is the logarithm

The height on each vertical line is ln|x|, and ln(−x) = ln x + iπ, so the −1 line is the +1 line carried half-way round the cylinder. On the closed circle, the angle of x above the horizontal is gd(ln x) = 2 arctan x − π/2. That function is the Gudermannian, the same one that turns Mercator height into latitude. Mercator's map is the logarithm of the stereographic coordinate.

The line you start with is centred on 0, its mirror is x ↦ −x, and its ends are ±∞. The lines you finish with are centred on 1, their mirror is x ↦ 1/x, and their ends are 0 and ∞. The animation carries the picture of addition into the picture of multiplication. The points that never move, ±1 on the line and the whole unit circle in the plane, are the pure directions: the sign in one dimension, the phase eiθ in two.

The fork: torus or Klein bottle

In the plane there are two natural ways to join a direction at 0 to a direction at ∞. Scaling, z ↦ λz, keeps each ray on itself, so it pairs the direction θ at 0 with θ at ∞. Gluing that way gives the torus drawn here. The reciprocal, z ↦ 1/z, sends the direction θ at 0 to −θ at ∞. Gluing that way gives a Klein bottle.

On the real line the two rules agree, because the only directions are 0 and π, and each is its own mirror image. The one-dimensional picture cannot tell a torus from a Klein bottle. The choice appears only in the plane.

Has this been done?

Each step is standard. Closing the line is the one-point compactification, drawn by stereographic projection, and the circle is the real slice of the Riemann sphere. Gluing two points of a circle gives the figure-eight, and gluing the poles of a sphere gives the pinched torus, whose round model is the horn torus. The separation is a real oriented blow-up, and the cut is the complex logarithm.

Wolfgang Däumler has written out conformal maps from the Riemann sphere and from the plane onto the horn torus, proposing the horn torus as a richer stand-in for the sphere. The smooth relative of this page's ring is the punctured plane folded by z ~ qz, the Tate presentation of an elliptic curve. Its real points are exactly two circles, the positives and the negatives, and as q → 0 it pinches to the horn torus. There, 0 and ∞ are never reached. Here they meet at a seam.
