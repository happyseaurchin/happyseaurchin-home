Tech-finance evolution

Which financial package paid for each technology to spread, and how long it took to come. Thirty-one technologies from farming to language models, on the same clock as Tech evolution, testing the waits in Front-wheel drive.

    What the record shows

    In years the wait shrinks. Against the time since, it holds.

    Farming waited 6,200 years for written ledgers, and the web seven years for the ad auction. But every interval shrinks as the clock nears the present. Measured against the time since each technology arrived, which is how Tech evolution's clock measures time, the waits show no downward trend. From 1500 to 1950 a package usually reached scale after about a sixth of that time. For the internet, the web and language models it took between three-tenths and four-fifths.

    Where the state was the buyer, there was no wait.

    Air power, synthetic fertiliser and nuclear deterrence had their package within a year of arriving and at scale within four, because the state paid for them through war and defence budgets. Powered flight had its first army contract within five years. Nuclear weapons never went near a market at all.

    Someone paid first, and half the time it was a ruler or a state.

    Of the 26 technologies with a known first payer, 13 were paid for first by a court, a crown, an army, a parliament or a public body. Patrons and single financiers paid for most of the rest: Johann Fust for Gutenberg, the Duke of Bridgewater for his canal, Drexel, Morgan for Edison.

    Booms come as a package scales.

    Front-wheel drive says the gap between a technology and its package breeds bubbles. In this record only two booms came before the technology had a package: the oil boom at Pithole in 1865 and the motor-company promotions of the 1890s. Every other boom came while the package was scaling or after, from the canals in the 1790s to the dot-com bust. The frenzy belongs to the package.

    Perez's rhythm holds. AI's place in it is open.

    Each of Carlota Perez's five surges shows its frenzy and crash here: the canals (1793), the railways (1847), the Baring crisis (1890), 1929 and 2000. Language models are paid for out of the largest firms' own profits, as Ford paid for mass production, with debt topping it up. Perez places AI inside the information surge, and in that reading production capital should lead, as it does. If AI is a sixth surge, a financial frenzy should follow, and the new debt forms would be its start.

    Language models: a package, and new forms forming

    The largest technology firms are paying for AI out of their own profits. Microsoft, Alphabet, Amazon and Meta spent over $251bn on capital in 2024 and about $400bn in 2025, and guided to roughly $720–760bn for 2026, with debt filling more of the gap each year. None of the new forms has yet taken a legal shape of its own, as venture capital did with the 1979 pension ruling.

      How this was made

      Each technology keeps its date from Tech evolution. For each one, five research passes on 14 October 2026 looked for who paid first and which financial package carried its spread, with three dates for that package: the first time it paid for the technology, when it took legal or institutional form, and when it carried the spread at scale. Where no financial form carried the spread, the record says so and why.

      Waits count from the technology's date in Tech evolution. A share of the time since divides the wait by the years between the technology's arrival and now, which is how the chart's clock spaces time: each step to the left is a longer stretch of the past.

      Dates before 1500 are approximate, and several pairings are judgement calls; each record gives its reason. The dot-com boom came before the web's ad auction but after its first venture funding (1995), so it counts as a boom of a scaling package.
