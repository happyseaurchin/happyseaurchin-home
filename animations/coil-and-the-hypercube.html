Glued, not folded

Coil's space and the folded hypercube are two different closed spaces: the 3-torus and the 3-sphere.

Coil is one cube whose opposite walls are one wall. The hypercube's net folds each of its eight cubes onto its neighbours. In both, ride straight along an axis and you come back in from the other side: that is the likeness you saw. But they differ the way a doughnut differs from a dice.

One dimension down: a doughnut and a dice

Glue a rectangle's opposite edges and it rolls up into a doughnut. Its four corners become one point where four right angles meet, so nothing has to close: the space it makes is flat.

Fold a dice's cross of six squares and only three squares meet at each corner. Folding has to close the missing quarter, the gold wedge. That closed gap is curvature: a dice is a sphere with its curving squeezed into eight corners.

Both are closed: walk off an edge and you come back on. But watch the two dots. On the doughnut they share one crossing. On the dice they set off together and meet again on the far face. Coil and the hypercube are this pair again, one dimension up.

Three straight rides meet again only in the hypercube

Set off from the centre along all three axes at once. In Coil each ride crosses one pair of glued walls and comes home, and the three share nothing but the start.

In the hypercube each ride passes through four cubes: out through a side cube, across the far cube, back through the opposite side cube. All three cross at the centre of the far cube, as lines of longitude meet at both poles.

Around each edge of the hypercube's skin only three cubes meet, the dice's gold wedge one dimension up. That is where its curvature lives, and why its straight paths come together again.

Coil can catch a loop; the hypercube can't

Stretch a rubber band along one of Coil's rides. It can slide round and round, but it always runs from a wall to the wall glued to it: it is caught around the space for good. Coil has three independent ways to catch a band, one for each pair of walls.

Put the same band in the hypercube and it slides off sideways into a side cube and shrinks to nothing. Every loop there can be shrunk, and that is exactly what makes the hypercube's skin a 3-sphere rather than a 3-torus.

Where the torus does hide in the hypercube

Take the sixteen squares where one of the four side cubes meets one of the column of four (the middle, top, far and bottom cubes). Together they make a board that wraps left to right and top to bottom: a torus, flat like Coil's walls, one dimension down. The two dots run on it just as on the board beside it.

That board splits the hypercube's skin into two solid rings, the four side cubes and the column of four, linked like two links of a chain. And there is one more torus here: glue the solid hypercube's opposite cubes, instead of folding its skin, and you get the 4-torus, which is Coil with a fourth direction to wrap around.

For the mathematically curious

Coil's space is the 3-torus: a cube with opposite faces identified, or ordinary space rolled up along a lattice. Its loops come in three independent kinds, one per axis, and none can be undone; its fundamental group is the group of integer triples.

The skin of the hypercube, like the skin of any solid in four dimensions, is the 3-sphere, and every loop on it shrinks to a point. The Poincaré conjecture, proved by Grigori Perelman in 2003, says that this one property pins it down: the 3-sphere is the only closed three-dimensional space in which every loop shrinks.

Counting does not tell the two apart. Corners minus edges plus squares minus cubes gives zero for both: 16 − 32 + 24 − 8 for the hypercube's skin, and 1 − 3 + 3 − 1 for Coil's single glued cube. One dimension down the same count does separate them: a dice gives 2, a doughnut 0.

The curvature sits on the edges. Three cubes around an edge leave a quarter-turn gap, as three squares do at a dice's corner. In Coil four quarter-corners meet around every edge, so Coil is flat everywhere, which is why it can be drawn as one undistorted cube.

The sixteen squares of the last picture are the hypercube's version of the Clifford torus. It cuts the 3-sphere into two solid tori whose core circles are linked once: a Heegaard splitting of genus one. The solid hypercube with its opposite cubes glued is the 4-torus: the cube is to the 3-torus as the hypercube is to the 4-torus.

Salvador Dalí painted the hypercube's net as a cross in Corpus Hypercubus (1954). Einstein's first model of the universe (1917) was a 3-sphere, and the 3-torus is a standard candidate for a flat universe of finite size; cosmologists have searched the microwave background for the repeated patterns it would leave.
