Cutting the line at zero and turning the halves

Mirror and Half-Turn

Cut the line at 0 and turn each half about its own unit, +1 and −1. Turn them in opposite senses and the picture stays a mirror image of itself. Turn them the same way and it is its own image under a half-turn. The torus has a cut for each.

Opposite turns

The positive half turns anticlockwise about +1 and the negative half clockwise about −1. Before the quarter-turn the lines cross below, on their continuations past the zeros. At the quarter-turn they are parallel and meet only at infinity. After it their far ends cross above, and the crossing comes down to where 0 was at the half-turn: one unit from each pivot, with the zeros out at ±2, four units apart. The thin curve is the bend that keeps 0 joined and ±1 fixed. At every angle its tangents at ±1 are the two lines.

Same turns

Both halves turn clockwise, so the lines stay parallel, 2|sin θ| apart. At the half-turn they lie along one line again, in the same picture the opposite turns reach, and the zeros meet again only at the full turn. The thin curve bends the same way with ±1 held fixed, and the zero has to split: at the quarter-turn 0⁺ joins −∞ and 0⁻ joins +∞.

Keeping one zero

The circle and the S

Now hold 0 at the centre and let ±1 move. Turned opposite ways, the halves close into a circle; the dashed arc is its positive half. Turned the same way, the positive half folds down instead of up, and the line becomes an S. At the quarter-turn the S is two semicircles of radius 1 inside a circle of radius 2, with +∞ one unit below +1 and −∞ one unit above −1. That is the dividing curve of the yin-yang, and the circle is one of its two small circles.

Notes

Two kinds of pair

The rigid turn is the bend's derivative

The bend that keeps 0 joined and ±1 fixed is a rotation of the Riemann sphere about the axis through ±1. In the plane its derivative at +1 is eiθ and at −1 it is e−iθ, with no stretching at either: exactly the two rigid turns. At the quarter-turn they are ×i and ×(−i), and the bend is the circle ℝ ∪ {∞}.

The cut on the first page agrees too. There each half became a vertical line at height ln|x|, which puts 0 infinitely far down: the extension below zero. The rigid turn gives height |x| − 1 instead, so 0 sits one unit below its pivot. Near 1 the two agree, since ln x ≈ x − 1, so the rigid line is the first-order shadow of both pictures.

Where the lines meet

Under opposite turns the two lines always cross on the vertical through the old zero, at height −tan θ. As θ grows from 0 the crossing drops below, passes through infinity at the quarter-turn, comes back from above and reaches the old zero at the half-turn. Over one full turn of the halves it goes twice round ℝ ∪ {∞}.

The half-turn is the same either way

Half a turn clockwise and half a turn anticlockwise end in the same place, eiπ = e−iπ, the identity that joined +∞ to −∞. So the opposite family and the same family pass through one shared picture at 180°. The bend there is x ↦ 1/x: infinity sits where 0 was, and 0 has gone out to the edges.

Same turns are not one motion

A Möbius map with two fixed points turns them by multipliers k and 1/k. For a rotation those are eiθ and e−iθ: always opposite turns. Equal turns at both points happen only with no turn at all, or at the half-turn. So a same-turn bend has to use a different map on each half, and something has to give.

Hold ±1 fixed and the zero splits. At the quarter-turn 0⁺ meets −∞ and 0⁻ meets +∞, the pairing made by x ↦ −1/x, which is a half-turn of the circle. The opposite turns pair 0 with 0 and ∞ with ∞, the pairing made by the mirror x ↦ −x.

Hold 0 instead and the pivots move. At the quarter-turn the halves are semicircles of radius 1 inside a circle of radius 2. Traced outward from 0 both curl the same way, each the other turned half a turn about 0. That is the yin-yang's dividing curve. The opposite turns, holding 0 in the same way, give one of its two small circles.

Folding up or down

On the last page the fold that brought +∞ round to −∞ could go up or down, and the choice decided which way round counted as positive. Here each half makes that choice for itself. Fold both far ends up and the halves must turn in opposite senses: the circle. Fold one up and one down and they turn the same way: the S. Folding to the same side takes opposite turns; folding to opposite sides takes the same turn.

Opposite spins travel, same spins revolve

The same split shows up in fluids. Two vortices of opposite spin move off together in a straight line. That pair is a smoke ring cut through its axis: the air goes the short way round the core, turning opposite ways in the two sections, and the ring travels. Two vortices of the same spin circle each other about their midpoint, and the arms they trail curl into an S. Mirror pairs travel; half-turn pairs revolve.

The torus cuts

A cut through the axis gives two circles side by side, mirror images across the axis. Going the short way round the torus they turn in opposite senses: the + and − circles of the first page. Tilt the cut and they become ovals, still side by side, until the plane touches the torus at two points. There the curves are true circles of the torus's large radius, and they cross at those two points. Yvon Villarceau wrote about them in 1848. Going the short way they still turn opposite; going the long way round they turn the same, and one is the other's image under a half-turn in the plane. Tilt further and the curves nest, one inside the other, turning the same way, until at the equator they are concentric circles.

The Villarceau cut is the hinge between the two kinds of pair. It is the one tilt of a flat cut through the centre where both senses of turning can be read at once.
