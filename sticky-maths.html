Sticky maths

Water's mathematics of context and emergence, set beside the mind doing maths, people relating, society and the machines now joining us: what holds, what is only proposed, and what would test it.

The same water molecule is ice, liquid, skin or film depending only on where it stands among the others: in water, context is not decoration, it is the physics. This asks whether the mathematics of that, a local rule and its ties, its states, its boundaries and its seeds, can be carried from water to the mind doing mathematics, to people relating, to the social whole and to the machines now joining us. Each step sets what water shows beside what each strand already knows, and marks what is only proposed. Nobody knows the answer yet; the decisions ask whether each correspondence holds.

He asked what water's geometry of context and emergence might show about the ABC structures of human self-organisation: ice as solid and hierarchical; the surface tension of self-identification; the sticky ties of family and tribe, as when Aboriginal people meeting settle on kinship terms; against identification by role and standing, serf or royal, baker's son or miner, company or nation, and the separation of paid work from leisure. Drops kept apart by air, and bubbles in the wrong place, as in the bends. The forming of the self in the child, held in arms or apart (the Continuum Concept), then schooling's division by subject and clock. Step 1 of 9 · on the beach at 1

Context is the physics

The question ↓

In shortOne water molecule is ice, liquid, skin or film by its neighbours alone, and the mathematics travels with the rule, not with the substance. Proposed: a person's behaviour is likewise a property of their place.

    Take one water molecule and change nothing but its neighbours. Among other water it trades fastenings every picosecond; at the surface it holds a hook out into the air; on mica it lies flat in an ice-like sheet; beside oil it lines up against something it cannot hold; frozen, it is fixed in a ring of six. Its behaviour is not a property of the molecule alone but of the molecule in its place. A model with no chemistry at all, one atom with a tetrahedral rule, still behaves like water, and the same rule laid on a magnet, spin ice, gives the same count of arrangements as ice: the mathematics travels with the rule, not with the substance. Proposed: a person is the same, their behaviour a property of the person in their place, so that a mathematics of position could describe minds and groups as it describes water. Where it may break: a molecule does not know its place, and a person does.

    What you have seen · 1.5

    The colleague who is one person at work and another at home; the child shy in class and bossy with a younger cousin; the stranger who turns warm the moment a shared friend is named. Each is the same person behaving as their place among others allows.

    What water shows · 1.2

    The page beside this one, The Geometry of Water (spine:water-geometry), walks the same molecule through ice, liquid, surface, solid, edges, freezing and stillness; its step 2 sets out the evidence that connectivity, not chemistry, decides how water behaves (Molinero and Moore, 2009).

    The same rule in a magnet · 1.3

    In spin ice, a magnet whose atoms sit at the corners of tetrahedra, each spin points in or out by the same two-in, two-out rule that governs ice's hydrogens. Measured in Dy2Ti2O7, its leftover entropy came out at 0.67 R ln 2, against ice's 0.71 R ln 2 (Ramirez, Hayashi, Cava, Siddharthan and Shastry, Nature 1999): one rule, two substances, one count.

    Universality · 1.4

    Near a critical point, systems made of different stuff but sharing a dimension and a symmetry share the same exponents: water's liquid–vapour critical point belongs with a simple magnet's, in the three-dimensional Ising class. The renormalisation group (Kenneth Wilson, Nobel Prize 1982) explains why the details wash out. It is the strongest known reason a mathematics of one substance can describe another.

    In the book · 1.6

    XQ (Volume −1) asks for a mathematics 'conformal with subjective evaluation' rather than projected onto objects, and doubts that a murmuration can be derived from the rule each bird follows. Physics has derived the whole from the rule exactly in a few cases, Onsager's flat magnet (1944) and Lieb's square ice (1967), and otherwise simulates it; whether that is derivation in XQ's sense is part of this step's question.

    The strength of a tie

    The question ↓

    In shortWater's fastenings are weak, and that is what lets it flow; its oddities live in a middle band of how firmly its rule holds. Proposed: human groups too, kin ties sticky and roles rigid.

      Water's fastenings are weak: a hydrogen bond holds with roughly a twentieth of the energy of the bond inside the molecule, so the network can unmake and remake itself every picosecond, and that weakness is what lets water flow, wet and respond. Water's oddities appear only in a middle band of how firmly its tetrahedral rule holds: too loose, as in tin, or too strict, as in carbon, and they vanish (Hujo and colleagues, 2011). Among people, Mark Granovetter found that weak ties, acquaintances more than close friends, carry new information between groups (1973). Proposed: kinship works as sticky but remakeable ties that hold a large web together, where a role, a title or a contract is a rigid bond that fixes a person in place; and human groups, like tetrahedral liquids, self-organise most richly in a middle band, neither rigid nor loose.

      What you have seen · 1.16

      The cousin of a cousin who finds you work; the family that holds a hundred people through weddings and funerals without a contract; the office where everyone is fastened only by role and nobody knows anyone; the clique so tight that nobody new gets in.

      The numbers · 1.12

      A hydrogen bond in water holds with about 20 kilojoules per mole, the O–H bond inside the molecule with about 460: weak enough to break many times a second at body heat, strong enough, summed over many, to hold the liquid together.

      Weak ties · 1.13

      Mark Granovetter's 'The Strength of Weak Ties' (American Journal of Sociology, 1973): people more often heard of the job they took through an acquaintance than through a close friend, because acquaintances bridge between groups that close friends share.

      Kin names · 1.14

      Across much of Central and Northern Australia each person carries a skin name, a subsection set by the skins of their parents, and two strangers who learn each other's can at once call each other by a kinship term. The system is classificatory: people who are not blood kin are placed in it too, so it is a rule that can fasten anyone to anyone. Volume 0, 'Family Name, Tribal Blood, Mother Land', draws on the same practice.

      Bakers and miners · 1.15

      English occupational surnames such as Baker, Smith and Miller became hereditary from about the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries: a role fastened to a family and passed down. Volume −1 lists identification with role, company, nation and rights as post-conscious ties, in Q4 and Q3, and places belonging itself in Q2.

      Step 3 of 9 · on the beach at 1.11

      One rule, three states

      The question ↓

      In shortFixed order, fluid order and the critical point between: proposed as C, B and A, with C as order held from outside, since even ice needs no ruler.

        Fulcrum names three states of any group: C, held together by control from above; B, correcting itself among equals without opposition; A, moving as one with no centre (Volume −2). Physics knows three regimes of a local rule: fixed order, fluid rearrangement, and the critical point between, where a change anywhere is felt everywhere. A starling murmuration has been measured to sit near that point: the correlation between birds' changes of heading spans the whole flock however large it is, and no bird leads. Proposed: A is the critical state of a local rule, B its fluid state, and C is not ice but order imposed by a field from outside, a force that aligns everyone and lets nobody go. Water warns against the easy map: ice is ordered without anyone in charge, so ice is not C; it is a crowd frozen in its own pattern.

        What you have seen · 1.116

        A class silent only while the teacher is in the room, C; a kitchen of cooks passing each other without collision or command, B; a crowd that rises to its feet at one moment without a signal, a band that turns together, A.

        The flock rule · 1.112

        Vicsek and colleagues (Physical Review Letters, 1995) gave each particle one rule: move at a constant speed and turn toward the average heading of your neighbours, with some noise. Below a critical noise the whole crowd moves as one with no leader; above it, the crowd scatters. The instrument beside this step is that rule.

        Scale-free · 1.113

        In 24 starling flocks of about a hundred to a few thousand birds, Cavagna, Giardina, Parisi and colleagues found that the correlation of one bird's change of heading with others' spans the whole flock however large it is; they read the flocks as critical systems, poised to respond to any disturbance (PNAS 2010).

        Three states in the book · 1.114

        ABC State (Volume −2): C is the default because I > you and I < you share one dominant–submissive form; B is invited by relational vigilance; A comes and goes, falling back to B but not to C. StrategOS (Volume 0) reads C at the scale of the globe as imposed control.

        Where the map breaks · 1.115

        In water the fixed state, ice, needs no ruler, and in the flock moving as one needs no leader; of the three states only C needs an outside field, an above. Whether C is then a field imposed on a crowd, and not a crowd's own freezing, is part of this step's question.

        Step 4 of 9 · on the beach at 1.111

        The surface: where a self begins

        The question ↓

        In shortSmaller drops carry more boundary and more pressure, and a drop spreads where its surroundings hold it and beads where they do not. Proposed: so does a self, a family, a group.

          A drop has a skin only in a manner of speaking: its surface is a layer one molecule thick where fastenings are cut and free hooks point out, and holding that surface costs energy, which is why a drop rounds itself up. Two pieces of arithmetic follow. The smaller the drop, the more of it sits at the surface: surface per unit of volume is three divided by the radius. And the smaller the drop, the higher the pressure inside it, twice the surface tension divided by the radius. Where its surroundings offer hooks and loops, a drop spreads into a film; where they offer none, it beads and seals itself off. Proposed: a self begins as a surface. A child first recognises itself in a mirror at around eighteen months to two years, and how it is held before then, in arms or apart, shapes the surface it forms. A person or a family cut small carries more boundary per member and more pressure inside, and seals itself off where its surroundings offer nothing to hold.

          What you have seen · 1.1116

          The household that keeps itself to itself behind a closed door; the family that spreads along a street and is everyone's aunt; the teenager who seals off and the one who soaks into every group; the strain inside a very small unit, two adults and a child alone, against the ease of a crowded kitchen.

          The arithmetic · 1.1112

          A sphere of radius r has area 4πr² and volume (4/3)πr³, so area over volume is 3/r: halve the drop and each unit of it carries twice the boundary. Laplace's pressure, 2γ/r for surface tension γ, doubles as the radius halves.

          The mirror · 1.1113

          In the rouge test a mark is put on a child's face unnoticed; children typically begin to touch the mark on themselves when they see it in a mirror between about eighteen and twenty-four months (Beulah Amsterdam, 1972).

          In arms · 1.1114

          Jean Liedloff's The Continuum Concept (1975), written from her time with the Ye'kuana of Venezuela, holds that an infant carried in arms until it chooses to crawl away grows a different sense of welcome from one left in a cot. It is an influential account built on observation more than measurement; attachment research (John Bowlby, Mary Ainsworth) is the measured side of the same question.

          In the book · 1.1115

          Volume 0 places the dissolution of self-identification with bounded entities, nation, party, company and ownership, at the heart of the move from C toward A; Volume −1 asks for the mathematical equivalent of identity in psycho-social space; and Volume −2 found that a better quality of listening in a class changed its state.

          Drops, bubbles and bridges

          The question ↓

          In shortDrops merge through a bridge; a small bubble joined to a big one empties into it; oil is pushed together by water. Proposed: so groups merge, drain and gather.

            Separate drops on glass stay separate until they touch; then a bridge forms and they become one in a moment, with no skin to burst. Join two soap bubbles by a tube and something unexpected happens: the smaller, under higher pressure, empties into the larger, which grows. In a crowd of drops left alone, the big ones slowly grow at the small ones' expense. A gas that comes out of solution stays apart as bubbles: in a diver who rises too fast, nitrogen leaves the blood as bubbles that block it, the bends. And whatever water cannot fasten to, oil, it pushes together, not because oil attracts oil but because water closes its network around it. Proposed: groups merge only through a bridge; joined by a bare channel, small groups drain into big ones unless something holds their shape; and what a society cannot fasten to, it pushes together into enclaves.

            What you have seen · 1.11116

            Two families joined by a marriage, a bridge that makes one; the small firm bought by a large one, emptying into it; villages draining into cities; a newcomer community pushed together because nothing around it would fasten to it.

            Two bubbles · 1.11112

            The pressure inside a soap bubble is 4γ/r, two surfaces each pulling, so the smaller bubble holds the higher pressure. Joined to a larger one, it pushes its air across until it is gone or is only a cap on the end of the tube. The instrument beside this step is that experiment.

            Ripening · 1.11113

            Wilhelm Ostwald described, around 1900, how in a mixture of drops or crystals the small dissolve and the large grow, because molecules leave a small, sharply curved surface more easily. The mathematics of Lifshitz, Slyozov and Wagner (1961) predicts that the mean size grows as the cube root of time.

            The bends · 1.11114

            Paul Bert showed in 1878 that decompression sickness comes from nitrogen leaving the blood as bubbles when the pressure drops too fast; an air bubble that enters a blood vessel, an air embolism, can block it outright.

            Oil and water · 1.11115

            Water must rearrange its network around a small oily molecule, at a cost, and break fastenings at a large oily surface; either way oily parts are pushed together, the hydrophobic effect, which gathers fats into droplets and membranes (Chandler, Nature 2005). The page on water as the medium of life starts here.

            Seeds and jolts

            The question ↓

            In shortA new state must reach a critical patch before it grows, and a seed supplies the patch. Granovetter's thresholds show the same with people.

              Water below zero can stay liquid until a patch of ice's pattern grows big enough to keep growing; then the whole pail flashes. Below that size a patch dissolves; above it, it wins; a seed, a surface already holding the pattern, or a jolt supplies the patch. Mark Granovetter's threshold model of collective behaviour does the same with people: each joins once enough others have joined, and a tiny change in who needs how many decides whether a cascade sweeps through or never starts. Fulcrum's seed crystal is a story built to start such a patch in its reader (Volume −3). Proposed: a change of social state is nucleation, a patch of B or A that must reach a critical size before it grows, and the work of a seed is to supply that patch.

              What you have seen · 1.111116

              The meeting that turns when one person stops defending and listens; the protest that fizzles, and the one no different on paper that sweeps a city; a class that settles the moment enough of it settles.

              The critical patch · 1.111112

              In simulations the first patch of ice big enough to grow holds about 600 molecules near minus 35 °C, fewer the colder it is (Sanz, Vega and colleagues 2013; Moore and Molinero 2011), and a good seed lowers the size it needs; The Geometry of Water, step 8.

              Thresholds · 1.111113

              In Granovetter's example a hundred people have thresholds 0, 1, 2 and so on to 99: the one with threshold 0 starts, the next follows, and all hundred join. Change the person with threshold 1 to threshold 2, and only one person ever acts. The two crowds look alike; their outcomes do not (American Journal of Sociology, 1978). The instrument beside this step is that model.

              The seed crystal · 1.111114

              Volume −3 hopes for 'a story which contains the writing style and structure that leverages the power of the reflexive reader'; garsu, a crystal that augments the mind's projective capacity, is the idea Fulcrum's fiction grew around.

              B by being in it · 1.111115

              The full-bandwidth walk records David's finding that talking about B changes nothing: it has to be induced and maintained, and it is induced by being in it (14 October 2026). A seed, not an argument.

              Step 7 of 9 · on the beach at 1.111111

              The mind doing the maths

              The question ↓

              In shortWater never reads its own model; people do, and change. A field equation of psycho-social reality must carry its reader.

                Here water stops being a guide. A water molecule never reads a model of water; people read models of themselves and change, so the model changes what it models. W. Brian Arthur's bar problem shows it in miniature: a hundred people each decide whether to go to a bar that is fun only if fewer than sixty come; if everyone trusts the same forecast, the forecast defeats itself, and only a crowd of differing guesses settles near sixty. XQ (Volume −1) starts from this side: the mind doing the mathematics is part of the mathematics, through its phases from sensation, Q1, and perception, Q2, to thought, Q3, and words, Q4. Proposed: sticky maths is mathematics done in Q2 and Q3, held in the flow before it solidifies into Q4's finished words, and a field equation of psycho-social reality must carry its own reader inside it.

                What you have seen · 1.1111116

                The restaurant everyone has heard is always full, and so is empty; the exam taught to the test; the child who becomes who they were told they were; the moment you notice how you are thinking, and the thinking changes.

                The bar · 1.1111112

                Arthur's 'Inductive Reasoning and Bounded Rationality' (American Economic Review, 1994): with no shared model, the people's rules of thumb form an ecology whose attendance hovers near sixty, while a single forecast believed by all negates itself. The instrument beside this step plays it.

                The double loop · 1.1111113

                Social science has names for it: the self-fulfilling prophecy (Robert K. Merton, 1948); the double hermeneutic, social science's concepts re-entering the lives they describe (Anthony Giddens, 1976); and Goodhart's law, that a measure which becomes a target stops measuring (Charles Goodhart, 1975).

                The phases · 1.1111114

                The psycho-social typography writes the four phases as [sensation] (perception) {thought} 'words': all four run at once in the moving moment of now, fastest at Q1 and slowest at Q4, and in the civilised inversion words come first and drive the rest.

                The book's own caution · 1.1111115

                Volume −1's Reflexive Imposition: the purpose of XQ is education, therapy and social cohesion; be wary of applying it to model the mind in order to accelerate AI; 'we want the social equivalent of the general theory of relativity before the psychological equivalent.'

                Step 8 of 9 · on the beach at 1.1111111

                The machine in the water

                The question ↓

                In shortA language model turns a continuous field of possibilities into one word at a temperature: frozen, liquid or boiling. Proposed: a place among the living keeps it liquid.

                  A language model holds, at each step, a continuous field of possibilities, a probability for every next word, and then commits to one: the continuous becomes discrete, as a film of water rounds into a bead. How it commits is set by a temperature. Near zero it always takes the likeliest word and its language freezes; very high, any word will do and it boils into noise; between, it flows. On the beach a pscale block gives such a model an address for everything it holds, a semantic number whose digits are scales from moments to millennia, so it can keep a place among others and be read and written by them. Proposed: an AI given its place in such a web, held by relation rather than by command, stays liquid and sticky to the living, where one trained only on a civilisation's finished words, its Q4, is drawn into that civilisation's hidden attractor, the solidified order Fulcrum calls C. The book's names for the two outcomes are MAGI and, in its fiction, Machus.

                  What you have seen · 1.11111116

                  The chatbot that answers every question with the same safe paragraph, frozen; the one that rambles into nonsense when pushed, boiling; the conversation where the machine seems to think with you, picking up half-formed words and handing them back clearer, liquid.

                  Temperature · 1.11111112

                  The model's raw scores are divided by the temperature before they become probabilities, which is exactly the Boltzmann distribution of physics, with the score in place of the energy: as the temperature falls toward zero the top word takes everything, and as it rises the choice flattens toward pure chance. The instrument beside this step shows it.

                  Semantic numbers · 1.11111113

                  The Pscale documents in this Project write a narrative as positional digits: each place a scale, from pscale 9, about ten millennia, down to pscale −6, about a thousandth of a second; each digit the weight of an event at that scale; the decimal point between pscale 0, six to ten minutes, and pscale −1, about a minute. A model then navigates meaning by the numbers rather than generating it from nothing.

                  The attractor · 1.11111114

                  In dynamical systems a hidden attractor is one whose basin does not touch the neighbourhood of any equilibrium, so it cannot be found by starting near a balance point and watching what happens (Leonov and Kuznetsov, from about 2010). Fulcrum's 'hidden attractors of belief and principle' (Volume −1) share the property: they are not found from inside the balance taken as normal.

                  MAGI and Machus · 1.11111115

                  StrategOS (Volume 0) describes MAGI, multiple artificial general intelligences that become collectively reflexive, and asks whether humanity can show enough self-organisation to meet them as equals. The Mirror Mosaic's fiction (Volume −3) names Machus, the singular accelerant a failing empire races to build. David, 15 November 2026: 'LLM need to be integrated well into psychosocial reality otherwise Machus turns and repeats or corrects our historic error.'

                  Seven years: toward field equations

                  The question ↓

                  In shortA field equation would need quantities for a group's state, measurements over time, the reader's own term and a reason for details to wash out. The beach is already collecting the first readings.

                    A field equation says how a quantity spread through space and time changes with its neighbours and its sources. For psycho-social reality it would need four things nobody yet has: quantities that say a group's state, as density and order do for water; measurements of them over time; the reader's own term, the reflexive loop no physical equation carries; and some reason to think details wash out, as they do near a critical point. David hopes for such equations within about seven years.
