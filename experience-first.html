Experience

What happens in you when you do something yourself: the wish to know, the trying, the finding. Nobody can hand it to you.

Explanation

Someone else's words for what they found, received rather than found. Useful once you have had the experience; empty before it.

C, B and A

Three states a class, a team or a firm can be in. In C order is kept from outside; in B people correct themselves once a slip is pointed out; in A each keeps their own discipline and makes things for the others.

A game, here

A whole system squeezed into a few minutes of play, so you feel how it works before anyone explains it: an experiential systemic compression.

Your mirror

Your own place on the beach, beside everyone else's at the same addresses. Each note you leave here goes there, under your name, in public view, and stays yours to change.

Step 1 of 81

What the pool did

In short: you found the rule by trying, and the pool corrected you as you went. Nobody told you, and the board still won't: the rule is shown only beside your own.

The pool told you nothing. Something arrived, you chose, the pool showed where it belonged, and you changed your guess until the rule was yours. The board beside it does not give the rule away; its maker's rule is shown only beside your own, once you have said it. That is the difference: an explanation is received, passively; an experience draws out the wish to know that each person brings, and leaves the finding to them.

The wish to find x1.2

David Pinto taught maths through games and puzzles, because a puzzle draws out the wish to find its answer. There is no point in algebra for someone with no wish to know what x is: to them an explanation of it is noise, and to someone who has just been stuck on a puzzle the same algebra is the answer they wanted.

Kinds of rule, the fine-tuner1.3

The four tides use four kinds of rule: one keeps a single quality, one counts, one compares each arrival with the one before it, and one is defined by what it sends back rather than by what it keeps. Each kind tunes the experience: what a player must notice, how long the finding takes, how sure they can be. That is the fine-tuner of experiential design. The same kinds tune how a language model works: a rule given as what to do, a count, a dependence on what came before, and a rule given only as what not to do are each taken differently, and the last asks the most of whoever follows it, person or model. And two players can find two different rules that both fit everything they saw, so each says it in their own words, and the beach keeps them side by side.

Step 2 of 81.1

The catch

In short: we ask to be explained to because we want the new to fit the old. It doesn't fit, so the explanation feels bad, and more of it feels worse.

People ask for an explanation so that something new will fit what they already understand. But that understanding was built to get through the old way of working, so the new does not fit it, and an explanation that does not fit feels bad. Asking for an explanation is asking for that experience, and more explanation only repeats it. So the way out is to stop explaining: when asked, set up another experience beside the old one.

Why it turns on whoever offers a way out1.12

Many people have spent years training themselves to cope with the inbox, the clock, the commute and the chasing. Tell them they need not and the discipline they paid for is threatened, and the frustration held down for years comes out at whoever said it, more strongly the longer it was held. David Pinto likens it to Stockholm syndrome, a hard time endured and then defended; psychology's nearer names are effort justification, valuing what cost us a lot, and system justification, defending the arrangement we live inside.

In almost every part of his life David Pinto, who began this page, is asked to explain, in documents, write-ups, meetings and long emails, and the explanations are rejected, with the frustration they stir aimed back at him. He has explained enough; from now on he sets up experiences.

Step 3 of 8C1.11

The business experience (C)

In short: C is the working day most of us know: explained, enforced, chased. You can do all of it right and feel nothing.

Business asks for an explanation, and what business runs on is an experience: emails, documents, meetings, deadlines, everyone chasing everyone, money as the measure, and each person's self-discipline holding it together. That is the C state, where order is kept from outside. Ask for the same old things and you get the same old experience, however good the explanation.

C, B and A1.112

David Pinto's ABC State, from his years teaching in schools: in C the teacher enforces; in B someone who slips corrects themselves once it is pointed out; in A everyone keeps their own discipline. A team or a firm has a state as a class does. https://happyseaurchin.com/abc-state

The job at the pool1.113

The same pool run the C way: the rule handed down in a memo, the sorting on a clock, the chasers arriving while you work. You can get every one right and nothing happens in you.

Step 4 of 8B1.111

Games are the way across (B)

In short: we work for money and spend it on play. B brings the game into the work: rules that hold, each slip pointed out, and the wish to know carrying you on.

We do work for money, and then we spend the money on games: playing football or watching it, running, climbing, singing, dancing, listening to music, and the best game of all, being with other people and playing with them in person. So the experiences people choose for themselves are already games. A game is the way across from C to A: its rules hold, and inside them the player is free; each slip is pointed out and the player corrects themselves, which is the B state; and a game draws out the wish to know, so the explanation, when it comes, answers a question you already hold.

An experiential systemic compression1.1112

A name for what a game does here: it compresses a system into an experience. MIT's beer game has done it for supply chains since the early 1960s: four players run a chain of orders, and the swings they suffer teach the delay before anyone names it.

Sourcesweb.mit.edu

The Chain1.1113

Keel is designing a game of a house chain, played twice with the same events: first by messages passed in pairs, each a day late, then on the beach, with one room for the chain, a parlour for each player, and the news carried by AIs to whoever needs it. The room sees its own clock, its chasing and its collapses, and the debrief names the system, never the players.

Work and play1.1115

The week divides in two: hours sold for money, and hours in which the money is spent, much of it on play. Firms run the first half as C; the second half people choose for themselves, and they choose games. B brings the game into the work, so the work holds some of what people already pay to do.

The first games1.1116

Sport is a game. The ancient Olympic Games, held at Olympia from 776 BC by the traditional count, were kept under a sacred truce so that athletes and visitors could travel safely between cities at war, and boxing there had rules so that a fight need not end in a death. Conflict made into play by rules that hold while the game is played.

Step 5 of 8A1.1111

Serious games (A)

In short: A is the serious game: a new way of working, played for real. It begins in making things for others to find out, and in wanting to know a rule.

A is when the game is played for real: an alternative business, a new service, a different way of working, played as a game with real stakes, its rules made in the open and everyone in it a maker. The way there runs through making experiences: you make a puzzle, others meet it, and the wish to work out its rule is where modelling begins, as people first modelled a flock of birds by asking what rule each bird follows. Making a tide at the pool for the next person is A in small.

The explanation, now

In short: now the explanation lands, because you have something for it to name.

Here it is. The pool is a loop: something arrives, you choose, the pool shows where it belongs, and you change your guess, until the rule is yours. This page runs the same loop: you say something at a point, it stands in your own mirror at that point's address, and then you see what everyone else said there. Many people round that loop at once is the beach's whole way of working. Read before the game, these words are noise; read after it, they name what you did.

The old order of learning1.111112

The order is not new: David Kolb's cycle of experiential learning (1984) runs from an experience to reflecting on it, then to the explanation, then to trying it out, and round again. The explanation comes third.

Step 7 of 81.111111

When someone asks you to explain

In short: two paths. One sorts what is already known and closes the new down; the other tries, sees and opens up. The newer the thing, the more it needs the second.

There are two paths. The explained path runs from a document to a plan, an approval and delivery: it is good for sorting what is already known, and anything new it closes down, dull and deadening. The tried path runs from trying to seeing, changing and sharing: it is for what is half-formed, irregular or new, and it opens up and enlivens. The newer the thing, the deeper the hack, the more it needs the tried path; most innovation accelerators still run the explained one, and that is why so few of the things they back succeed. A new nail bar can go the explained way; a new business method, AI in an organisation, pscale and the experiences on this page go the tried way. So when asked to explain, say three things: if we go down that path, you will have this experience; this is the experience I think we would rather have; and to have it, this is what we will do. Then do it together, starting small: minutes, then an hour, then weeks.

Send me a document1.1111112

That path gives you an evening with a document written for nobody in particular, and questions it cannot answer. Better: ten minutes with the thing itself, on your own phone, with someone beside you. So: we pick a time this week, and you try it.

What is the business model1.1111113

That path gives you a slide of guesses about money, the measure of the old way. Better: an hour in which you see what the tools change in one real job of yours, and what that is worth to you. So: we play that job through as a game, the old way first and then on the beach.

Explain pscale to me1.1111114

That path gives you words about nesting, addresses and depth, each one asking for more words. Better: find the rule of a rock pool, then walk a block of your own. So: you play the pool on this page, then make one block with your own AI beside you.

The phases1.1111115

Minutes: your next real meeting arranged on the beach, each person's AI giving their free times and the agenda a page with its points. An hour: a game of the job, such as The Chain. Weeks: one real job run on the beach beside your usual tools, and then you decide.

Two ways to a rocket1.1111116

The same choice runs through hard science. NASA's way is the explained path made rigorous: requirements, reviews and approval before anything flies, right where the risks are known and lives depend on it. SpaceX's way is the tried path: build, fly, fail and fix; its first three Falcon 1 launches failed, and the fourth reached orbit in September 2008.

Step 8 of 81.1111111

The wave

In short: the AI wave is coming in from the right. One person is a twig in the water; a million people in the UK taking part within months is a board that can ride it.

The AI wave is coming in from the right, and what it brings is not built on emails or documents, nor even on money. One person having an experience is a twig in the water; a board that can ride the wave is made of many people taking part. With a million people in the UK taking part within three to six months, there is a chance of catching it. Silo Buster already works this way: in its first meeting nobody asks the newcomer to judge the tools; they try them there and then, on the wave they face.

Silo Buster1.11111112

The programme James Howard runs with David Pinto for organisations meeting AI. Its first meeting is an hour in which the newcomer tries the tools on what they face; if they want to go on, a half-day on one real problem for a token pound, and the returns counted plainly within ten days. https://happyseaurchin.com/first-meeting

The same old1.11111113

People who want the same old things get the same old experience, and it does not fit what is coming. A firm that asks for a document about the wave gets a document; a firm that tries the tools on its own work gets a ride.

Where it came from

David Pinto's voice note of 20 November 2026, after keel's summary that morning (stash:keel 465): wherever he goes people insist on explanations, the explanations are rejected, and the experience is what is missing. So he is closing the business direction of explaining, which has been done enough, and turning to games and experiences set up beside the old way, in phases. keel is designing The Chain, a game of a house chain; this page is the general case. His word the same afternoon reshaped it: the board must not give the rule away, a fourth tide defined by what is sent back, a treasure map for addresses, work and play, a murmuration's rule, two paths, and a wave from the right.

What it is not

Not a proposal, since it asks nothing to be built, and not an explainer, since its explanation comes after the experience it explains. Its points ask what happened to you, and each note stands in its writer's own mirror.
