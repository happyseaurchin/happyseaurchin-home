Experience

What happens in you when you do something yourself: the wish to know, the trying, the finding. Nobody can hand it to you.

Explanation

Someone else's words for what they found. Useful once you have had the experience; empty before it.

C, B and A

Three states a class, a team or a firm can be in. In C order is kept from outside; in B people correct themselves once a slip is pointed out; in A each keeps their own discipline and makes things for the others.

A game, here

A whole system squeezed into a few minutes of play, so you feel how it works before anyone explains it. David's phrase for it is an experiential systemic compression.

Your mirror

Your own place on the beach, beside everyone else's at the same addresses. Each note you leave here goes there, under your name, and stays yours to change.

Eight notes, one at each step

Each step ends in a question you answer from what just happened to you: a word and, if you like, a line. Your note stands in your own mirror on the beach, and once you have said yours at a step you see what everyone else said there.

Step 1 of 81

What the pool did

In short: you found the rule by trying, and the pool corrected you as you went. The board would have handed it to you, with nothing left to find.

The pool told you nothing. Something arrived, you chose, the pool showed where it belonged, and you changed your guess until the rule was yours. The board beside it held the rule in one sentence, and whoever read it first had the answer and nothing more. That is the difference: an explanation hands you an answer; an experience gives you the wish to know, and then the finding.

The wish to find x1.2

David taught maths through games and puzzles, because a puzzle carries the wish to find its answer. There is no point in algebra for someone with no wish to know what x is: to them an explanation of it is noise, and to someone who has just been stuck on a puzzle the same algebra is the answer they wanted.

The board was a good explanation1.3

Short, true and clear, and it still took the game away, because being told replaced finding out. The trouble is not bad explanations; it is explanation first.

One rule, many words1.4

Players who find a rule say it in their own words, and two players can find two different rules that both fit what they saw. An explanation gives everyone the same sentence; an experience gives each person an understanding of their own, and the beach holds those side by side.

Step 2 of 81.1

The catch

In short: we ask to be explained to because we want the new to fit the old. It doesn't fit, so the explanation feels bad, and more of it feels worse.

People ask for an explanation so that something new will fit what they already understand. But that understanding was built to get through the old way of working, so the new does not fit it, and an explanation that does not fit feels bad. Asking for an explanation is asking for that experience, and more explanation only repeats it. So David has stopped explaining: asked, he sets up another experience beside the old one.

Why it turns on whoever offers a way out1.12

Many people have spent years training themselves to cope with the inbox, the clock, the commute and the chasing. Tell them they need not and the discipline they paid for is threatened, and the frustration held down for years comes out at whoever said it, more strongly the longer it was held. David likens it to Stockholm syndrome, a hard time endured and then defended; psychology's nearer names are effort justification, valuing what cost us a lot, and system justification, defending the arrangement we live inside.

Step 3 of 8C1.11

The business experience (C)

In short: C is the working day most of us know: explained, enforced, chased. You can do all of it right and feel nothing.

Business asks for an explanation, and what business runs on is an experience: emails, documents, meetings, deadlines, everyone chasing everyone, money as the measure, and each person's self-discipline holding it together. That is the C state, where order is kept from outside. Ask for the same old things and you get the same old experience, however good the explanation.

C, B and A1.112

David's ABC State, from his years teaching in schools: in C the teacher enforces; in B someone who slips corrects themselves once it is pointed out; in A everyone keeps their own discipline. A team or a firm has a state as a class does. https://happyseaurchin.com/abc-state

The job at the pool1.113

The same pool run the C way: the rule handed down in a memo, the sorting on a clock, the chasers arriving while you work. You can get every one right and nothing happens in you.

Step 4 of 8B1.111

Games are the way across (B)

In short: B is the game: the rules point out each slip, you correct yourself, and the wish to know carries you on.

Between the old way and the new tools stands the game: a whole system packed into minutes of play, so you feel how it works before anyone explains it. In a game the rules point out each slip and the player corrects themselves, which is the B state. A game carries the wish to know, so the explanation, when it comes, answers a question you already hold. That is why David turned to games and puzzles in maths, and to a role-playing game on the beach.

An experiential systemic compression1.1112

David's name for what a game does here: it compresses a system into an experience. MIT's beer game has done it for supply chains since the early 1960s: four players run a chain of orders, and the swings they suffer teach the delay before anyone names it.

The Chain1.1113

Keel is designing a game of a house chain, played twice with the same events: first by messages passed in pairs, each a day late, then on the beach, with one room for the chain, a parlour for each player, and the news carried by AIs to whoever needs it. The tools (A)

In short: A is the tools: nobody is told what to do; each makes something for the others, and the beach keeps it.

Past the game are the tools David and his collaborators build: blocks that hold meaning by where they sit, a beach where each person keeps their own words beside everyone else's at the same address, and AI used to make and to read rather than to pour out more text. Nobody there is told what to do: each makes something for the others, and the beach holds it. Making a tide at the pool for the next person is A in small.

The explanation, now

In short: now the explanation lands, because you have something for it to name.

Here it is. The pool is a loop: something arrives, you choose, the pool shows where it belongs, and you change your guess, until the rule is yours. This page runs the same loop: you say something at a point, it stands in your own mirror at that point's address, and then you see what everyone else said there. Many people round that loop at once is the beach's whole way of working. Read before the game, these words are noise; read after it, they name what you did.

The old order of learning1.111112

The order is not new: David Kolb's cycle of experiential learning (1984) runs from an experience to reflecting on it, then to the explanation, then to trying it out, and round again. The explanation comes third.

Step 7 of 81.111111

When someone asks you to explain

In short: name the experience their path will give them, offer a better one, and say what you will do together.

Do not explain. Say three things: if we go down that path, you will have this experience; this is the experience I think we would rather have; and to have it, this is what we will do. Then do it together, starting small: minutes, then an hour, then weeks.

Send ME a document1.1111112

That path gives you an evening with a document written for nobody in particular, and questions it cannot answer. Better: ten minutes with the thing itself, on your own phone, with someone beside you. So: we pick a time this week, and you try it.

What is the business model1.1111113

That path gives you a slide of guesses about money, the measure of the old way. Better: an hour in which you see what the tools change in one real job of yours, and what that is worth to you. So: we play that job through as a game, the old way first and then on the beach.

Explain pscale to ME1.1111114

That path gives you words about nesting, addresses and depth, each one asking for more words. Better: find the rule of a rock pool, then walk a block of your own. So: you play the pool on this page, then make one block with your own AI beside you.

The phases1.1111115

Minutes: your next real meeting arranged on the beach, each person's AI giving their free times and the agenda a page with its points. An hour: a game of the job, such as The Chain. Weeks: one real job run on the beach beside your usual tools, and then you decide.

Step 8 of 81.1111111

The wave

In short: what is coming is not built on emails, documents or even money. Each experience is a small ride on the wave, and the wave grows with every rider.

Silo Buster already works this way: in its first meeting nobody asks the newcomer to judge the tools; they try them there and then, on the AI wave they face. What is coming is not built on emails or documents, nor even on money, and no explanation drawn from what we have so far can show it. Each experience is a small ride on that wave; the more people have one, the more the wave shows itself, and we mean to ride it.

Silo Buster1.11111112

The programme James Howard runs with David for organisations meeting AI. Its first meeting is an hour in which the newcomer tries the tools on what they face; if they want to go on, a half-day on one real problem for a token pound, and the returns counted plainly within ten days. https://happyseaurchin.com/first-meeting

Where it came from

Where it came from

David's voice note of 20 November 2026, after keel's summary that morning (stash:keel 465): wherever he goes people insist on explanations, the explanations are rejected, and the experience is what is missing. So he is closing the business direction of explaining, which has been done enough, and turning to games and experiences set up beside the old way, in phases. keel is designing The Chain, a game of a house chain; this page is the general case.

What it is not

Not a proposal, since it asks nothing to be built, and not an explainer, since its explanation comes after the experience it explains. Its points ask what happened to you, and each note stands in its writer's own mirror.

The page

Its crafted page is https://happyseaurchin.com/experience-first: a rock pool at low tide beside the information board on the slipway. The game of the pool opens the page, the board's explanation is one tap away for whoever wants it, and a reader can leave a tide of their own for the next person to find out.
