# `animations/fields-factories-services.html`

Fields, factories, services (24 Nov 2026), at `/animations/fields-factories-services`: keel's chart of ten of today's rich economies from 1800 to 2025, each drawn as the shares of its output from agriculture, industry and services (value added at current prices, the three summing to 100), headed 'Sweden left the land some 70 years after Britain, then followed the same arc'. Britain and Sweden stand as two large stacked landscapes, fields at the bottom, the brick town in the middle and sky above. Eight more stand beneath them as small multiples: the US, France, the Netherlands, Belgium, Finland, Spain, Japan and South Korea. Then come one sector across all ten as lines beside a ranked list of bars, the table of the year, and the notes on how the series are built. One year moves every chart, set by the play button, the slider, a hover or drag on any chart, or the arrow keys (Shift for ten years). Play runs at 16 years a second from 1800 and starts by itself after a moment, unless motion is reduced, which opens the page at 2025. 'Copy all data as CSV' copies every row; where copying is blocked it shows them in a read-only box (`#csv`), which is not a write box and so carries no dictation. The data is written into the page (`DATA`: each country's rows of year and three shares, with a flag for the World Bank's years). It is the long-run series of Herrendorf, Rogerson and Valentinyi (2014), updated with the GGDC 10-sector database and compiled by Our World in Data, joined to the World Bank's current value-added shares rescaled to 100 (from 2002 to 2017, depending on the country). The join is drawn as a labelled step ('World Bank from 2012') and not chain-linked. Where the two sources overlap they differ by up to six points, which keel read as a change of ruler, not of economy (`stash:keel` 486). For the same reason South Korea's highest industry share, in 2011, the long-run series' last year, is not named as its peak in the page's words, which give Spain's 1974 as the latest. The page reads nothing from the beach and loads nothing from outside but Google Fonts' Red Hat Display and Red Hat Text; it follows the reader's light or dark.
