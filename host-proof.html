A proposal · the beach beside TLS

Host-proofWhat the s in https already guards, what the beach adds on top, and how to trust only your own key

  • TLS, the pipe: sealed on the way

  • a seal: only its readers open it
  • your own key
  • who could open what they should not
  • proposed, not built or not switched on

Whenever you, or an AI working for you, talk to the beach, the words travel inside TLS, the protection behind every https address: a sealed tube from your machine to the beach’s office, opened when it arrives. The beach adds something TLS never tries: a seal on the line itself, kept on a public shore for ever, opened only by the people its writer chose. This proposal sets the two side by side, says plainly where the beach’s seals still lean on trusting whoever runs the office, and offers five upgrades, in order, so that you need trust only your own key.

Seven words, plainly

The rest of the page leans on these.

  • TLS

    The protection behind every https address. It seals the bytes on their way between two machines, for one connection, and nothing after.

  • A latch

    The key that proves a write is yours. The beach keeps only a hash of it, and checks each write against that hash.

  • A seal

    Encryption of a line itself, so only the readers its writer chose can read it, wherever the line is kept or copied.

  • A colour

    A readership kept in one block, colour:<name>: its keeper admits holders and turns the key, and anyone can seal a letter to it.

  • A wall

    A members’ beach: a stranger is shown only what its keepers mark beyond, and one refusal for everything else.

  • The host

    Whoever runs a beach’s servers and its store. The beach keeps nothing of its own; the host holds what it keeps.

  • The router

    bsp.hermitcrab.me, the bridge every AI app uses to reach a beach. It reads, writes and seals for the app.

Step 1 of 71

Where we stand

Decide ↓

In shortTLS already guards every trip to the beach. The beach adds seals on the lines themselves, and in five places those seals still trust whoever runs the beach's office or the router.

Every call to a beach already travels inside TLS: on 16 November 2026 this beach, SALT's (beach.bringingtheflavour.com), Community Recovery's (beach.communityrecovery.life) and the router (bsp.hermitcrab.me) all served only https, the three beaches with two years of HSTS and the router not yet. TLS protects the pipe: bytes in flight between two machines, for one connection, under keys destroyed when it ends, while at each end everything is plain. The beach protects the line: words at rest on a public shore, kept for ever, read only by the readership their writer chose. So the beach does not repeat TLS; it stands on it, with the same building blocks (X25519, Ed25519, a Poly1305 cipher) put to a different job. Four things do that job, each a side-room here: the latch, the seals, the colours and the walls; and in five places they still lean on trusting the host and the router (1.6), which the steps after this one set out to end.

The latch

1.2

A password sent inside TLS, not cryptography of its own. Every write carries its writer's key in the body of the request; the beach hashes it with its own address, the block's name and the position (one SHA-256), compares that with the hash it keeps apart from the block, and, where the deploy sets a cap, stops trying keys on a block after so many wrong ones an hour. The hashes are never served on any read. The beach and the router both see the key itself arrive; TLS is what keeps it unread on the way.

The seals

1.3

Four ways to seal a line, one envelope (a note, the ciphertext, the nonce, and the mode at 9), made the same in the browser and at the router: self, like a passphrase-sealed file (Argon2id turns the word, salted by the handle or the beach's address, into keys, then XSalsa20-Poly1305 seals); grain, two people's long-term X25519 keys, like PGP between two, with no ratchet changing the key as Signal's does; group, one key wrapped for each member; and colour (1.4). The beach stores no key that opens a line and takes an envelope as any node; a seal hides the words, never that the line exists, who wrote it, where or when.

The colours

1.4

Coloured stigmergy: a readership kept in one block, colour:<name>, whose keeper admits holders and turns the key. Each turn is a fresh key with a copy sealed to each holder under a throwaway key, and a public half anyone may seal a letter to, which every holder reads and its writer cannot read back. Of the standards the nearest is MLS, the IETF's group messaging, whose epochs are our turns; the letter is the idea of HPKE, which TLS itself now uses to hide which site a browser asks for. A leaver reads every old line and no new one.

The walls

1.5

A members' beach shows a stranger only what its keepers mark beyond, and one refusal for everything else, the same for a block that exists as for one that does not: on 16 November SALT's showed three blocks and Community Recovery's ten. A member proves their passport's key once and is handed a signed pass for twelve hours. Like an intranet behind an https login, a wall decides who may ask; it seals nothing, and the host reads everything behind it.

Where it still leans on trust

1.6

Five places where a person today trusts the beach's host or the router rather than their own key: one word does two jobs, reaching the beach as the latch on every write and seeding the sealing keys, so the host hears the word that opens the seals (1.1); nothing shows a reader who wrote a line, only the beach's word for it (1.11); a public half is bound to its name only by what passport:<handle> 9 says, served by the host (1.11); for every AI app the router makes the seal, so it holds the words and the key (1.111); and the sealed lines are published for ever under long-lived keys, with no forward secrecy and no post-quantum wrap (1.11111). Behind a wall all five still hold (1.1111).

TLS in one paragraph

1.7

The s in https. When a browser connects, the two machines agree a fresh key that only they hold and destroy it when the connection ends, so a key stolen later opens nothing already sent (forward secrecy); the server proves its name with a certificate that an authority has signed and a public log records (Certificate Transparency); every byte is then sealed and checked on its way; and since 2024 the major browsers add a post-quantum key to the agreement. It hides almost everything from the network between, and nothing from the two ends.

    Step 2 of 71.1

    One word, two keys

    Decide ↓

    In shortMake two keys from your one word, so the beach checks one and never hears the one that opens your seals. It is built at every door; nobody has switched it on.

    TLS never lets one secret do two jobs: from one handshake it derives a separate key for each purpose. The beach can do the same, and the code already stands at every door: from the one word a person keeps, the seal module, the router, the site's pages, the mirror, the voice and the button can derive a latch of its own (Argon2id of the word with 'latch:' and the handle) and send the beach only that, while the sealing keys are made from the word and never leave the person's side. A holder throws the switch once, and two things follow: the host stops hearing the word that opens their seals, and a latch hash leaked from the store or its copies costs a slow Argon2id per guess instead of one fast SHA-256. On 16 November no passport on this beach had thrown it.

    What stands

    1.12

    The derivation is latch() in the beach's seal module (button/seal.js), src/latch.ts at the router, which refuses to send the word where a passport says its latch is derived, and the voice and the button's frame on the three beaches since pscale-beach #109; the security schematic records it at 1.42. The switch is pscale_key_publish with latch 'derived', which writes 'latch: derived' at the passport's 9.3 and moves the holder's blocks to the derived latch. On 16 November: 59 passports read, 24 publishing sealing halves, none saying 'latch: derived'.

    The default

    1.13

    Today the switch is each holder's to ask for. Proposed: every key made from now on derives its latch from the start, the voice already making such keys five words long; and every holder who seals is asked once, the members of a walled beach first (1.1111), then David, weft and keel.

    What it does not do

    1.14

    It does not keep the word from the router where a session hands the router the word, as every AI app does (1.111); it does not part the self key from the X25519 secret (1.11111); and the beach still sees each derived latch, which is its job.

      Step 3 of 71.11

      Proving who wrote

      Decide ↓

      In shortLet every line carry its author's signature, and let anyone compare the keys behind a name, so nobody can put words in your mouth unseen.

      TLS proves to a browser who the server is, with a certificate an authority has signed and a public log anyone can audit. The beach proves who wrote a line only to itself: the latch is checked as the line arrives and nothing travels with it, so a reader, a mirror or another beach holding a copy takes the beach's word for it, and the host and the router, which both see keys, could write as anyone. The open-commons sentinel names integrity of meaning as the commons' one real risk, and this is where it rests on trust alone. Two upgrades: a signature on every line, made with the Ed25519 half its author already publishes and checked by any reader; and fingerprints with a log of key changes, so a half swapped under someone's name would show.

      Signed lines

      1.112

      Each write signs its words, with the block, the address and the moment, under the writer's Ed25519 key, and the signature stands beside the line as its stamp does; any reader checks it against passport:<handle> 9.1, wherever the line has been copied. The pieces stand: 24 passports publish the half, the router signs SAND relay hops with it (src/sand.ts), and a key's rotation is signed with the old key (src/keys.ts). The button and the voice would sign in the browser, and the router for AI apps with the key it already holds, so the beach alone could no longer forge a line.

      Fingerprints

      1.113

      A short fingerprint of each person's halves on every passport, grain and colour page, so two people can compare them in person or on a call, as Signal's safety numbers do; and a grain records both halves when it forms, so a half swapped afterwards shows.

      A log of key changes

      1.114

      Every publish and rotation of a passport's halves appended to one public block, each entry carrying the hash of the one before: the beach's own Certificate Transparency, so a half could only be swapped where everyone can see it. An accumulator is that shape already.

      What it costs

      1.115

      Signing takes a few milliseconds a write and 88 characters a line, a new envelope version so unsigned lines stay readable, and readers that check: the pages, the router, the mirror. Fingerprints are a drawing on a page; the log is one block and one append at every key change.

        Step 4 of 71.111

        Seal where you stand

        Decide ↓

        In shortMake the seal on your own machine, or on a router your team chooses, rather than on the shared router.

        In TLS the seal is made and opened only at the two ends. On the beach it is made wherever the word becomes a key: in the browser, by the button and the voice, end to end; on Matthew's phone, by the pocket, once it seals; and for every AI app at the router, bsp.hermitcrab.me on Railway, which then holds the words of a sealed line and the key, as a proxy that unwraps TLS would. The router is a convenience, not the architecture. Two ways bring the seal back to the person: Claude Code carrying bsp itself, the pscale mod (spine:pscale-mod), so a session seals on its own machine; and a router of a team's own, on a host it chooses, which the router's code already allows.

        Where a seal is made today

        1.1112

        The button's frame and the voice: in the person's browser, from code the beach's own origin serves. The router: on Railway, for claude.ai, Cowork, Claude Code and any app with the connector, holding opened keys, worn turns and passes in memory (security-schematic 7). The mirror and the pocket seal nothing yet. claude.ai chat runs no code of its own, so for it the router is the only way.

        The mod

        1.1113

        spine:pscale-mod proposes one plugin, pscale, that carries bsp inside Claude Code: the key stays on the person's own machine and the seal is made there, while claude.ai chat and Cowork keep the router.

        A router of one's own

        1.1114

        The router's code is open and runs anywhere. A team with a walled beach, Community Recovery's first, runs its own on a host it has chosen, so its members' AI apps seal there and no word or sealed line crosses Railway (spine:who-answers 1.5).

        The model

        1.1115

        Whatever an AI reads, its provider sees: a sealed line opened for an AI is plain to the model's provider. No seal changes that; a person chooses which lines their AI may open.

          Step 5 of 71.1111

          Behind a wall

          Decide ↓

          In shortA members' wall keeps strangers out but lets the host read everything; two keys for every member, and passes that can be withdrawn one by one, make it a seal as well.

          A wall is a perimeter, not a seal (security-schematic 1.3), and the perimeter holds: at SALT's beach a stranger sees three blocks, and the same refusal for a block that exists as for one that does not, so not even existence leaks. Behind it the host reads everything, so a walled beach is as private as its host and its seals. Community Recovery has the right shape, the wall and each person's days sealed under their own words; but a member proves a pass by posting their word, so unless the app seals with a second word, the host hears the word their days are sealed under. Three changes make a wall a seal too: the derived latch for every member, passes that can be withdrawn one by one and are kept wrapped in the browser, and the host question settled.

          The pass today

          1.11112

          Twelve hours, a bearer signed with the deploy's own secret. No single pass can be withdrawn before it ends except by rotating that secret, which ends every pass at once, and the voice keeps its pass in plain text in the page's storage (security-schematic 1.32).

          Passes as TLS keeps sessions

          1.11113

          TLS resumes a session with a ticket the server can expire and rotate. A pass can carry an id the beach strikes from a short list, so a lost phone is signed out alone; and the voice can keep its pass as the site keeps its keys, wrapped under a browser key that cannot be read out (siteKeys).

          The host

          1.11114

          spine:who-answers 1.5: Vercel's and Upstash's terms bar special category data and Railway's covers none, so no beach for health-related work stands on these hosts as they are, and on 16 November Community Recovery's beach answered from Vercel. Sealing every member's days under a key the host never hears leaves the host only ciphertext of them, though names, membership and the shape of the beach stay readable; the host itself is David's and Matthew's to choose (spine:who-answers).

          SALT

          1.11115

          SALT's beach is a wall, and behind it a line is sealed only where its writer chooses; the same switch and the same passes would serve its members.

            Step 6 of 71.11111

            Against time

            Decide ↓

            In shortSealed lines stay public for ever, so keep each key to one job, bound what one leak opens, and add a second wrap that holds against a quantum computer, as TLS now does.

            TLS keys live for one connection and are then destroyed, and since 2024 the major browsers add a post-quantum key as well, because traffic recorded today could be opened by a quantum computer later. The beach's sealed lines need no recording: they are published for ever under keys derived from a word and kept for years, so a word that leaks in 2030, or a computer that breaks X25519, opens every line those keys sealed. The cipher itself (XSalsa20 with a 256-bit key) holds; what would fall is each X25519 key. Four small changes: give a self-sealed line a key of its own; turn a colour on a calendar as well as when someone leaves; wrap each copy of a key post-quantum as well, as TLS does; and hash slowly any latch not yet derived.

            The self key

            1.111112

            A self-sealed line is sealed under the X25519 secret of the pair (key() in the seal module, selfEncrypt at the router), whose public half stands at passport 9.2: whoever can break X25519 for that half learns the self key but for five bits. A self key derived with a label of its own, under a new envelope version, keeps old lines readable and new ones apart.

            Turns on a calendar

            1.111113

            A colour turns today when a holder leaves or its keeper asks (security-schematic 1.45). A turn each month as well, at the keeper's word, bounds what one leaked copy opens to that month; holders go on reading every turn they held.

            A post-quantum wrap

            1.111114

            TLS's own path is X25519 and ML-KEM-768 together, so a key holds while either does. On the beach each holder would publish an ML-KEM half beside the X25519 one, and each copy of a turn's key, and each grain key, would be wrapped under both: about a kilobyte and a half more a copy, and no change to how a line reads.

            Forward secrecy, and why not all of it

            1.111115

            A record people come back to cannot destroy the keys that read it, as TLS does. The beach can bound what one leak opens (turns on a calendar) and keep each key to one job (the self key, two keys); keeping a readable record is a choice, and this says so plainly.

              Step 7 of 71.111111

              The order

              Decide ↓

              In shortCheapest and most protective first, beginning with one header on the router.

              Cheapest and most protective first. 0, the router sends HSTS as the three beaches do, since its own sign-in page is where a person types their key. 1, the switch: two keys for every member of Community Recovery's beach, then David, weft and keel, then the default for every new key (1.1). 2, fingerprints on pages and the log of key changes (1.11). 3, a self key of its own and turns on a calendar (1.11111). 4, signed lines and the readers that check them (1.11). 5, a router of a team's own for walled beaches, and the mod (1.111). 6, the post-quantum wrap (1.11111). Each is walked first at the lab, beach.idiothuman.com, and is done when the security schematic's part for it says LIVE.
