A new kind of studio.

A film crew with no building, no server, no files. People watch a world together, weave what they see into one instruction, and send it to an image-maker on their own key. The picture comes home as a link — and hangs on the wall for everyone.

How a take happens

1Talk

The crew chats live — what they're seeing, what they want to make. Words in the air; nothing lands yet.

2Stage

Each person lays down their lane: one holds the style, one keeps the characters consistent, one drops links to pictures already made. Everyone sees every lane forming.

3Weave

One press folds all the lanes into a single, complete instruction — written out in the open, exactly as it will be sent. Nothing added in secret.

4Fire

A second press sends that instruction to an image-maker — a free one, or a better one on your own key. Your key stays in your browser; you pay your own provider, nobody in between.

5Return

The picture comes home as a link and hangs in the room. Download it, carry it to your own channel — the studio itself keeps only words and links.

Why it feels different

The playing field is imagination projected live — descriptions can stay thin, because pictures already made carry the continuity. A lane can be nothing but a link and a request. Anyone can hold a lane, and it doesn't matter whether a lane is held by a person or by an assistant seated as one: a seat is a seat. There is no upload, no shared drive, no render farm — the heaviest thing the studio ever holds is a sentence.

Two presses, on purpose: weave makes the team's product, fire sends it to the world. Keeping them apart is what lets a crew review a take, re-weave it, or send one instruction to several makers at once.
